## Overview

Legislative gridlock is the failure of the lawmaking system to reach agreements that change the status quo on issues before it. An agenda-based measure asks what share of salient issues remains unresolved within a defined period. This differs from counting laws: the same number of enactments can represent substantially different performance when the agenda changes.

## Key Ideas

- **Specify the denominator.** Binder measures failed salient agenda items divided by all salient agenda items in each two-year Congress. The result depends on how potential enactments and issue salience are identified.
- **Distinguish sources of disagreement.** Divided executive-legislative party control, ideological separation between chambers, and polarization within Congress are different constraints. Unified party control does not guarantee agreement between the House and Senate.
- **Separate preferences from procedural hurdles.** Bicameral consent and supermajority procedures shape whose agreement is needed. Their consequences depend on the distribution of preferences; a nonsignificant filibuster coefficient in one aggregate model does not establish procedural irrelevance.
- **Treat agenda construction as measurement.** Editorial attention can reveal proposals that never become laws, but newspaper selection, attention thresholds, and judgments about partial enactment affect the estimated failure rate.
- **Avoid conflating levels and values.** System-level agenda resolution differs from individual [[legislative-effectiveness|Legislative Effectiveness]]. More enacted legislation also need not imply better policy or representation.
- **Keep historical associations bounded.** Binder's 1947-1996 study finds comparatively robust associations for divided government and bicameral distance, while some other findings change with the salience threshold. Those estimates do not by themselves establish effects in other eras or institutional settings.

## Important Papers

- [[the-dynamics-of-legislative-gridlock-1947-96|The Dynamics of Legislative Gridlock, 1947-96]]: constructs an agenda denominator and tests partisan, institutional, and policy correlates of unresolved issues.
- Mayhew (1991), *Divided We Govern*: the enactment-count comparison discussed by Binder.
- Krehbiel (1998), *Pivotal Politics*: a supermajority-centered theoretical account discussed in Binder's study.

## Related Concepts

- [[legislative-effectiveness|Legislative Effectiveness]]: progress by individual lawmakers rather than aggregate resolution of the policy agenda.
- [[political-polarization|Political Polarization]]: ideological divisions can affect the availability of legislative compromises.
