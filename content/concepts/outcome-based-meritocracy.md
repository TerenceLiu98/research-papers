## Overview

Outcome-based meritocracy allocates rewards or opportunities according to observed past success while treating that success as a proxy for underlying talent or effort. The approach becomes unreliable when outcomes also depend strongly on opportunity, noise, inherited resources, or other external conditions. In that setting, rewarding winners can convert an initially accidental advantage into cumulative advantage.

## Key Ideas

- **Outcome is not a pure measure of merit:** Observed performance can combine ability, effort, opportunity, measurement error, and chance. A ranking of outcomes does not identify their separate contributions without additional assumptions or evidence.
- **Selection can amplify noise:** When prior success determines future resources, favorable shocks increase both the current outcome and access to later opportunities. This creates a feedback process related to the Matthew effect.
- **Ex ante and ex post merit differ:** A person selected after an exceptional outcome may not have had the highest probability of succeeding before the outcome occurred. Extreme observations can be especially sensitive to luck and unreliability.
- **Alternative rules have different objectives:** Equal, lottery-based, threshold-lottery, and mixed allocations may broaden access, while performance-based rules may exploit information about demonstrated capacity. Their comparison requires explicit welfare goals, costs, and evidence about predictive validity.
- **Simulation is diagnostic, not dispositive:** A model can show how winner-based allocation fails under specified dynamics. It cannot establish the prevalence of those dynamics or identify the best real-world policy without empirical validation.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/talent-vs-luck-the-role-of-randomness-in-success-and-failure|Talent vs Luck: The Role of Randomness in Success and Failure]] (Pluchino, Biondo, and Rapisarda, 2018): calls success-based reward allocation "naive meritocracy" and compares it with broad and random funding in a stylized model.
- Merton (1968), "The Matthew Effect in Science": cited foundation for cumulative advantage in scientific recognition.
- Denrell and Liu (2012), "Top performers are not the most impressive when extreme performance indicates unreliability": cited argument that extreme observed performance need not identify the strongest underlying performer.
