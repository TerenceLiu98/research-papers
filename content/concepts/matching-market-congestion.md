## Overview

Matching market congestion occurs when decentralized search creates work that does not produce additional matches. Applicants may send applications that employers never screen, while employers may screen and make offers to applicants who have already matched elsewhere. The friction is especially important when availability is not observable and screening or contact is costly.

## Key Ideas

- **Congestion can burden either side.** In a screening-limited market, abundant low-cost applications reduce applicant availability when offers arrive, so employers incur repeated screening costs and some exit. In an application-limited market, employers screen but applicants send privately beneficial applications without internalizing the competition and unread applications they create.
- **Market balance changes the welfare loss.** Applicant over-application is most damaging when applicants are abundant relative to employers. Screening waste becomes central when many applicants eventually match and employers frequently encounter unavailable candidates.
- **Application volume is a design variable.** Caps can increase offer acceptance and reduce unread applications. A well-chosen cap may improve both sides when screening congestion otherwise deters employer participation.
- **Equivalent volumes need not imply equivalent welfare.** Raising application costs can induce the same application volume and employer welfare as a cap while directly lowering applicant welfare. A platform-set wage may reproduce the same aggregate welfare as a cap under particular equilibrium conditions, but transfers change its distribution.
- **More contact need not mean more useful matching.** Additional applications can weakly increase matches while still lower welfare because the marginal contacts consume application and screening effort.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/managing-congestion-in-matching-markets|Managing Congestion in Matching Markets]] develops a dynamic mean-field model, identifies screening-limited and application-limited regimes, and compares application caps, application costs, and platform-set wages.
- Coles et al., "The Job Market for New Economists: A Market Design Perspective" studies constrained signals of interest in a congested market.
- Fradkin, "Search, Matching, and the Role of Digital Marketplace Design in Enabling Trade" provides cited empirical motivation about uncertain availability in a digital marketplace.
