## Overview

Alliance burden-sharing is the distribution of military contributions and defense costs among alliance members. It shapes both the alliance's aggregate capabilities and each member's dependence, autonomy, and influence. An asymmetric distribution can therefore do more than redistribute expense: under particular strategic conditions, it can deter outsiders, restrain allies, or induce additional allied investment.

## Key Ideas

- **Capabilities and influence are linked.** A member that supplies more protection can increase an ally's dependence and gain leverage over its military choices.
- **Asymmetry does not identify free riding by itself.** A weaker member may contribute less because a stronger member is subsidizing it, but calibrated protection can also be designed to induce the weaker ally to build the forces needed for deterrence.
- **Restraint and deterrence require different conditions.** In Iwanami's model, a heavy superpower contribution restrains a potentially revisionist ally when that ally's armament cost is relatively low. When the cost is high, more limited support induces allied rearmament and deters a non-signatory.
- **Aggregate power and unilateral threat differ.** An alliance may gain total capabilities without increasing an outsider's exposure to unilateral revision if the potentially revisionist member remains too weak to act alone.
- **Domestic constraints matter strategically.** Economic limits, legal restrictions, and public opposition can raise the cost of military investment, shifting which burden-sharing arrangement can sustain the status quo.
- **The effects are conditional.** Complete information, credible commitments, move order, intervention rules, and the specification of military costs can determine whether burden-sharing produces restraint, deterrence, free riding, or no alliance.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/asymmetric-burden-sharing-and-the-restraining-and-deterrence-effects-of-alliances|Asymmetric burden-sharing and the restraining and deterrence effects of alliances]]: endogenizes alliance formation and arms buildup in a three-player game, deriving separate restraining and deterrent regions and illustrating them with postwar Japan.
- Olson and Zeckhauser (1966), "An Economic Theory of Alliances": treats collective defense as a public good and explains disproportionate major-power contributions and incentives for smaller allies to free ride.
- Morrow (1991), "Alliances and Asymmetry": relates asymmetric alliances to exchanges between security and autonomy.
- Fang, Johnson, and Leeds (2014), "To Concede or to Resist? The Restraining Effect of Military Alliances": analyzes how dependence on an alliance can promote concessions and restraint during crises.

## Related Concepts
