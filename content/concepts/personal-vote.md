## Overview

The personal vote is the portion of a candidate's electoral support attributable to personal qualities, activities, qualifications, or record rather than the party label alone. Electoral rules shape incentives to cultivate this support by determining whether voters can select individual candidates, whether co-partisans compete for votes, and how much control party leaders retain over nomination and rank order.

## Key Ideas

- Ballot structure affects both supply and demand. Candidate-centred rules encourage politicians to advertise local ties, competence, incumbency, and other personal attributes, while giving voters a way to reward them. Party-centred rules make an ordered party list the principal object of choice.
- Intraparty competition is a central mechanism. Under open-list proportional representation and STV, co-partisans may need to differentiate themselves even while benefiting from a shared party brand and coordinated campaigning.
- District magnitude does not have a mechanically uniform effect. Larger districts can increase the number of co-partisan competitors and incentives for personal differentiation, but they can also weaken direct voter-candidate ties and make the party label more useful as a heuristic.
- Personal and ideological considerations can coexist. [[Electoral systems and ideological voting]] reports that ideology predicts choices under all electoral systems in its sample, although it has less weight under candidate-centred rules.
- Candidate-centred rules may improve perceived personal representation and satisfaction while reducing party-voter ideological congruence. Evaluating reform therefore requires attention to multiple dimensions of representation.
- Personal-vote incentives can also affect nomination, party cohesion, legislative behavior, constituency service, and the kinds of candidates parties recruit; observed preference voting captures only one stage of this broader process.
