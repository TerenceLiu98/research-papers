## Overview

Public goods games model situations in which individuals contribute to a shared pool and the resulting benefit is distributed among the group. Each participant can gain from others' contributions while avoiding the personal cost, creating a free-rider tension between individual and collective incentives.

## Key Ideas

- **Synergy creates the dilemma.** In the standard formulation, each cooperator pays a cost, contributions are multiplied by a synergy factor, and the benefit is shared by all group members. A group's total welfare can rise with cooperation even when an individual defector has a short-run payoff advantage.
- **Group size changes the threshold.** Larger groups dilute each contribution under equal sharing, so the synergy factor needed to favor cooperation depends on group size and on the population structure.
- **Payoff and imitation are separate mechanisms.** The game specifies payoffs; an update rule specifies who is observed and copied. Individual-biased selection can favor defectors, while group-biased selection can favor groups whose average payoff is lifted by cooperation.
- **Higher-order structure is substantive.** Hyperedges preserve which participants share one game. Overlapping groups can support group-level selection and information transfer that a pairwise projection would not represent.
- **Simulation outcomes are conditional.** Cooperation levels depend on mutation, selection strength, group membership, and update rules. Results from a public goods model should not be treated as direct measurements of cooperation in a population.
