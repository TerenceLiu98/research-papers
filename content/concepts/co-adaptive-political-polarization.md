## Overview

Co-adaptive political polarization describes a reciprocal process in which public political attitudes respond to media environments while media content, platforms, and technologies adapt to public preferences. The process treats technological change as partly endogenous: media can shape polarization, but a polarized public can also create demand and incentives for more polarized media.

## Key Ideas

- Public and media polarization form a feedback system rather than a one-way causal chain. Audience behavior affects what media producers and platforms supply, which then alters the environment to which audiences respond.
- Reciprocal adaptation does not necessarily amplify polarization. When neither side has a directional bias, adjustment between media and the public can dampen exogenous shocks and lead them toward a shared equilibrium.
- A persistent public bias toward polarization changes the feedback loop. Media adapt toward biased public preferences, and the public subsequently adapts toward the changed media environment, allowing small directional tendencies to accumulate.
- The speed of adaptation matters. Slower media adjustment to audience demand can reduce the frequency and magnitude of polarization increases.
- Empirical study requires treating media polarization as multidimensional, potentially combining partisan content, market fragmentation, selective-exposure affordances, recommender behavior, and network homophily.
- The framework extends reinforcing-spiral accounts by making reciprocal media-public adjustment explicit in a dynamic model.
