## Overview

Party cues are signals of partisan affiliation that help voters evaluate political alternatives. An explicit party label can summarize expected policies and performance or activate an affective attachment. Voters may also infer affiliation from candidate positions and characteristics, so omitting the label does not necessarily remove partisan information.

## Key Ideas

- **Reciprocal inference:** Party can signal unobserved policy positions, while observed policies can signal party. Responsiveness to either source alone does not uniquely identify a voter's underlying motive.
- **Incremental information:** A label-revelation experiment estimates the additional effect of making affiliation explicit given the other information presented. A small effect can coexist with strong reliance on inferred party.
- **Realistic profiles:** Preserving within-party correlations can make candidate comparisons more plausible. It also makes individual attribute effects model-dependent when attributes are not independently randomized.
- **Policy and identity can coexist:** Remaining responsiveness to voter-candidate proximity is compatible with substantial party influence. Party-based choices can reflect policy preferences beyond the issues shown in an experiment.
- **Group and context dependence:** Effects can differ across past party voters and political moments. An average label effect should not substitute for relevant subgroup contrasts.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/the-effects-of-party-labels-on-vote-choice-with-realistic-candidate-differentiation|The effects of party labels on vote choice with realistic candidate differentiation]]: randomizes label visibility for profiles benchmarked to British candidates; finds asymmetric label effects and a strong predictive role for candidate party even when labels are hidden.
- Kirkland and Coppock (2018), "Candidate choice without party labels: new insights from conjoint survey experiments", as discussed in the paper above.
- Orr, Fowler, and Huber (2023), "Is affective polarization driven by identity, loyalty, or substance?", cited there for the difficulty of separating partisan identity from inferred policy substance.

## Related Concepts
