## Overview

The liberal international order refers to the institutions, norms, and cooperative arrangements associated with the US-led order developed after World War II and expanded after the Cold War. In Aryal's synthesis, its components include economic openness, multilateral rules, security cooperation, international society, and liberal-democratic aspirations. The concept is contested, particularly over how liberal principles relate to hegemonic leadership.

## Key Ideas

- US predominance and liberal institutional arrangements are analytically distinguishable. Declining hegemonic influence does not logically establish that all associated norms and institutions disappear.
- States may benefit from international markets and institutions while contesting their distribution of authority. Participation, selective resistance, and demands for reform can occur together.
- Greater representation for emerging powers could change decision-making within existing institutions, but it does not automatically preserve their liberal commitments.
- Institutional persistence, liberal normative content, and Western leadership are separate outcomes that assessments of order should specify.
- Aryal interprets Asian emerging powers as seeking greater influence within the order. This is a substantive argument about their incentives, not part of the concept's definition.

- Mearsheimer (2019), "Bound to fail: The rise and fall of the liberal international order." Discussed by Aryal as a critique of the post-Cold War liberal project.
