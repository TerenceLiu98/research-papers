## Overview

Conditional party government links strong majority-party leadership to two conditions: ideological separation between parties and ideological cohesion within the majority. Separation supplies incentives to pursue a distinct partisan agenda, while cohesion makes delegation to party leaders more attractive and feasible.

## Key Ideas

- **Both conditions matter.** Between-party distance and within-majority homogeneity are separate theoretical ingredients; a polarization measure alone does not capture the entire argument.
- **Operationalize distinct dimensions.** The state legislative effectiveness study measures polarization as distance between party ideological medians and majority heterogeneity as the standard deviation of majority members' ideal points.
- **Examine substantive influence.** Majority-minority differences in legislative effectiveness and the share of majority members outperforming the minority median measure advantages in advancing proposals, complementing analyses of voting or formal leadership powers.
- **Distinguish electoral incentives.** The insecure-majorities hypothesis instead emphasizes incentives created by close competition for chamber control. It can complement conditional party government, but it is a distinct explanation.
- **Preserve the evidentiary boundary.** Associations between cohesion, polarization, and effectiveness gaps support observable implications without independently identifying a causal delegation mechanism.
