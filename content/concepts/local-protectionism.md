## Overview

Local protectionism favors firms or economic activity within a jurisdiction at the expense of exchange with other jurisdictions in the same country. It can operate through procurement decisions and barriers to nonlocal firms rather than explicit tariffs. Observed home bias alone is insufficient evidence of inefficient discrimination: transport costs, service requirements, firm quality, and other economic differences can also favor local suppliers.

## Key Ideas

- **Political rivalry provides one mechanism.** In [[concepts/promotion-tournaments|Promotion Tournaments]], a transaction benefiting another jurisdiction can improve a rival official's career prospects. Fang, Li, and Wu model this as a reason to reject some higher-quality outside projects in favor of local alternatives.
- **Reduced bilateral exchange and home favoritism are distinct observations.** Fewer contracts or investments going to one rival could represent diversion to a third city. Evidence of increased home-city allocation helps establish the local-protectionism interpretation in a setting with multiple destinations.
- **Selection thresholds reveal potential inefficiency.** The tournament model predicts a higher quality threshold for competing-city firms. Larger capital, assets, or profits among winning outsiders are empirical proxies for this prediction, not direct measurements of welfare loss or project performance.
- **Procurement discretion and firm ownership matter.** The Chinese evidence shows larger strength-gap coefficients for non-public procurement and local-SOE investment. Private investment has a weaker association, and the central-SOE estimate is nonsignificant.
- **Institutional boundaries define the relevant rivals.** Fang, Li, and Wu find their promotion-gap pattern within provinces and report nonsignificant cross-province tests. This limits the tested mechanism; it does not establish that cross-province trade barriers are absent.
- **Networks and career concerns can change allocation.** Shared factional backgrounds are associated with more inter-city exchange, while approaching a mayor's change of office is associated with less. These observational patterns are consistent with the model's affinity and promotion-weight channels.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/tournament-style-political-competition-and-local-protectionism-theory-and-evidence-from-china|Tournament-style political competition and local protectionism: Theory and evidence from China]]: explains within-province protectionism through promotion rivalry, with evidence from procurement, equity investment, and home-city allocations.
- Young (2000), "The razor's edge: distortions and incremental reform in the People's Republic of China": a study cited by Fang, Li, and Wu in motivating internal barriers.
- Barwick, Cao, and Li (2021), "Local protectionism, market structure, and social welfare: China's automobile market": a cited comparison on protectionism and welfare in a specific industry.
