## Overview

Weather exposure and corporate performance concerns how short-run, location-specific weather affects firms' revenues, costs, earnings, and market expectations. The relevant exposure is determined by operating geography rather than headquarters alone: firms may own establishments across many climate zones, and each location can contribute differently to production or sales. Because effects can differ by industry, season, and temperature range, aggregate linear estimates may conceal offsetting gains and losses.

## Key Ideas

- **Exposure follows economic activity.** Gridded weather can be linked to establishments and aggregated using sales, employment, or other weights. Each weighting scheme encodes a different claim about which locations matter for firm outcomes.
- **Responses can be nonlinear and bidirectional.** Extreme heat or cold may cross operational or behavioral thresholds. A shock can reduce earnings in one industry while increasing earnings in another, so a small population-average effect need not imply low exposure.
- **Season and industry are part of the treatment definition.** The same absolute temperature can represent ordinary weather in one place or season and an extreme in another. Demand for travel, dining, utilities, clothing, and recreation can also respond differently across the calendar.
- **Revenue and cost channels should be separated.** Weather may shift consumer visits and purchases, labor supply and productivity, energy needs, input costs, or agricultural output. Revenue, cost of goods sold, and overhead responses help discriminate among these mechanisms but rarely identify them uniquely.
- **Short-run weather is not long-run climate change.** Weather variation can identify immediate sensitivity, while long-run climate exposure also includes adaptation, relocation, capital replacement, supply-chain reorganization, and equilibrium price changes.
- **Physical exposure and information processing are distinct.** A weather event may predict cash flows without producing an immediate forecast or price response. Later incorporation could reflect gradual learning, managerial disclosure, or other correlated information.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/temperature-shocks-and-industry-earnings-news|Temperature shocks and industry earnings news]] links establishment-level U.S. temperature exposure to quarterly earnings. It documents industry-season heterogeneity, emphasizes consumer-demand and labor-productivity channels, and finds little immediate analyst or stock-price response to intra-quarter shocks.
- Addoum, Ng, and Ortiz-Bobea (2020), "Temperature shocks and establishment sales": reports negligible population-average effects on U.S. establishment sales and productivity, illustrating how aggregation can mask heterogeneous responses.
- Pankratz, Bauer, and Derwall (2023), "Climate change, firm performance, and investor surprises": examines firm performance under extreme heat outside the United States.
- Graff-Zivin and Neidell (2014), "Temperature and the allocation of time: implications for climate change": connects temperature to time allocation and labor supply, providing a key productivity mechanism.
