## Overview

AI adoption in research concerns whether and how researchers integrate AI tools into scientific work, including ideation, analysis, coding, writing, and management. Access, familiarity, frequency of use, proficiency, and research outcomes are distinct quantities. Frequent use or perceived helpfulness alone does not establish better science.

## Key Ideas

- **Measure the workflow as well as its outputs.** Surveys can capture exploratory and managerial uses absent from publication or code records, while introducing recall, self-report, and participation biases.
- **Distinguish adoption from competence.** In the German researcher survey, widespread reported use coexists with a 21.0% success rate among attempts at a single prompting task. Neither metric substitutes for a broad assessment of effective AI use.
- **Familiarity and beliefs are separable.** The survey associates gender differences in adoption primarily with familiarity rather than skepticism or perceived returns. A descriptive decomposition does not identify what a familiarity-building intervention would change.
- **Institutional conditions may matter.** Learning orientation, legal uncertainty, and access to suitable tools are associated with use or reported as barriers. Their causal importance requires intervention or longitudinal evidence.
- **Perceived policy is not actual policy.** Respondents can report institutional rules that do not exist, making awareness and verified regulation different exposures.
- **Specify the technology and task.** A survey covering AI broadly cannot isolate generative AI or particular systems. Comparisons across tools, disciplines, or survey waves require attention to question wording and sample composition.
