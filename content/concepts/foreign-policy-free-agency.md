## Overview

Foreign policy free agency describes how individual lawmakers can challenge established party positions and cooperate across party lines on international issues. In Homan and Lantis's account, progressive and conservative legislators may share objections to executive excess despite substantial ideological disagreement. It is a propensity expressed in particular contexts, rather than a fixed caucus membership or a general preference for moderation.

## Key Ideas

- Ideological extremity and independence from party establishments are distinct proposed sources of free agency.
- Personal commitments, factional affiliations, and political opportunities can activate cooperation around particular issues, such as congressional control of war powers.
- A cross-party coalition can form at ideological extremes. Observed bipartisanship therefore does not necessarily indicate centrist convergence.
- Homan and Lantis operationalize potential free agency by adding a party-oriented first DW-NOMINATE dimension to the second dimension, interpreted as anti-establishment orientation. More negative composite scores indicate greater potential; this interpretation requires substantive validation.
- Coalition participation, pivotal voting power, and achieved policy change are separate outcomes. Descriptive score differences among voting blocs establish neither causal mechanisms nor forecasting performance.
