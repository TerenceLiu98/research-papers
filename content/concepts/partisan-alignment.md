## Overview

Partisan alignment, in the voter-district sense, is the match between an individual's party affiliation and the party favored by the electoral district. A voter can be aligned or misaligned in an equally uncompetitive district, making alignment distinct from closeness of competition. It is also distinct from policy proximity between a voter and a candidate, studied under [[concepts/ideological-voting|Ideological Voting]].

## Key Ideas

- **Relational measurement:** The same district is aligned for one party's supporters and misaligned for the other's. A measure therefore combines individual affiliation with district partisan composition.
- **Competitive districts:** A design may classify closely balanced districts separately. Fraga, Moskowitz, and Schneer use a PVI interval from D+5 through R+5; this is a study-specific threshold rather than a universal definition.
- **Competing predictions:** [[concepts/expressive-voting|Expressive Voting]] and greater mobilization of co-partisans can predict higher turnout under alignment. Partisan threat can predict the opposite. A pure competitiveness account does not by itself predict different turnout responses in equally lopsided aligned and misaligned districts.
- **Boundary-induced variation:** Redistricting can change alignment without residential relocation. Measuring both maps with fixed historical election returns avoids defining treatment using post-treatment voting behavior, although strategic boundary selection and bundled electoral changes remain identification concerns.
- **Participation and representation:** Alignment may affect whether people vote, not only who wins their votes. A turnout benefit for one party's supporters does not establish a net benefit for the whole electorate or justify a districting plan.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/partisan-alignment-increases-voter-turnout-evidence-from-redistricting|Partisan Alignment Increases Voter Turnout: Evidence from Redistricting]]: Uses stationary registrants and the 2012 congressional redistricting cycle; preferred pooled estimates show about a 1.7-percentage-point increase when moving from misalignment to alignment, with weaker evidence for the reverse transition and in midterms.
