## Overview

Policy feedback treats policy as a cause of subsequent politics. Benefits, administrative encounters, and interpretations of government can change who participates and how people understand their political interests.

## Key Ideas

- Resource effects change material or civic capacities and participation costs; interpretive effects change perceptions of citizenship, deservingness, and government.
- Program design matters: universal and means-tested programs may generate different experiences, including stigma or opportunities for civic incorporation.
- Registration, turnout, attitudes, and policy protection are distinct outcomes. A persistent registration gain alone does not establish a mobilized constituency.
- Policies may mobilize opponents as well as beneficiaries. Aggregate effects do not automatically identify which group responded.
- Separating feedback from selection requires credible comparisons of how outcomes would have evolved without the policy.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/the-politics-of-policy-the-initial-mass-political-effects-of-medicaid-expansion-in-the-states|The Politics of Policy]]: finds persistent registration gains following Medicaid expansion, weaker short-term turnout evidence, and no detectable turnout effect in 2016. Enrollment-linked registration is a suggested mechanism rather than an identified mediator.
- Pierson (1993), “When Effect Becomes Cause: Policy Feedback and Political Change,” and Campbell (2012), “Policy Makes Mass Politics,” provide theoretical foundations cited in that study.
