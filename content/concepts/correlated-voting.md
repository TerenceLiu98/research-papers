## Overview

Correlated voting occurs when voters' choices are statistically related rather than independent. Shared evidence, communication, common beliefs, opinion leaders, and schools of thought can make votes move together; opposing perspectives can produce negative correlations. The concept matters because majority accuracy depends on the joint distribution of votes, not only on each voter's marginal accuracy.

## Key Ideas

- Pairwise uncorrelatedness is weaker than independence. A group can have zero pairwise covariance while its joint voting pattern still undermines majority accuracy.
- For voter indicators \(X_i\), the average joint probability \(\bar{r}\) and average pairwise correlation \(\bar{\rho}\) summarize interdependence when marginal accuracies are held fixed.
- With average accuracy \(\bar{p} > .5\), a sufficiently low average interdependence gives a sufficient condition for the majority to outperform the average voter.
- When individual accuracies are equal, the variance of the average vote rises with average correlation. Under additional well-behaved distributional assumptions, this makes majority accuracy decrease as average correlation increases.
- Negative correlations can improve aggregation by spreading errors across voters, but they do not compensate for an average accuracy at or below .5.
- Common information and communication can create positive dependence, while opposing schools of thought can create negative dependence. The institutional problem is therefore to combine useful diversity with enough accuracy.
