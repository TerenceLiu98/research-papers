## Overview

The Define–Combine Procedure (DCP) is a two-stage method for drawing electoral districts. If a jurisdiction needs $N$ districts, one party defines $2N$ contiguous, equal-population subdistricts. A second party then combines adjacent pairs into the final $N$ districts. Each party acts independently and in its own interest, but neither controls the complete map.

The mechanism adapts fair-division logic to redistricting. Its intended advantage is institutional: partisan incentives remain, yet the sequence of moves limits how completely either party can translate control into seats.

## Key Ideas

- **Separated powers:** The Definer controls the initial boundaries, while the Combiner controls which adjacent subdistricts become final districts.
- **Strategic anticipation:** The Definer moves first and must choose a plan while anticipating the Combiner's seat-maximizing response. In the idealized finite game, the outcome can be analyzed by backward induction.
- **Geographic constraint:** Requiring paired subdistricts to be contiguous restricts the Definer's ability to distribute favorable voters freely across final districts.
- **Process-based fairness:** DCP constrains how maps are produced rather than requiring a court or commission to select one numerical definition of partisan fairness.
- **No cooperation assumption:** The mechanism does not require compromise or a neutral tie-breaker; its checks arise from opposing unilateral choices.
- **Compatibility, not automatic compliance:** Other constraints can be imposed before or during the process, but DCP does not itself decide legal questions such as the required number of majority-minority districts.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/a-partisan-solution-to-partisan-gerrymandering-the-define-combine-procedure|A Partisan Solution to Partisan Gerrymandering: The Define–Combine Procedure]] introduces DCP and evaluates it with congressional-map simulations, analytical models, and enumerable grid maps.
- Pegden, Procaccia, and Yu (2017), "A Partisan Districting Protocol with Provably Nonpartisan Outcomes," presents a related adversarial protocol with formal guarantees.
- Landau, Reid, and Yershov (2009), "A Fair Division Solution to the Problem of Redistricting," develops an earlier fair-division approach to districting.
- Ely (2019), "A Cake-Cutting Solution to Gerrymandering," proposes another cake-cutting mechanism for partisan districting.
