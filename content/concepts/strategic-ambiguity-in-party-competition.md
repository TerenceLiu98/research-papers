## Overview

Strategic ambiguity in party competition is the deliberate choice to leave a party's promised policy position imprecise. It adds the breadth or dispersion of a platform to its location as a strategic choice. Ambiguity can help leaders accommodate audiences with different expectations, but its value depends on how those audiences form beliefs and reward the leader.

## Key Ideas

- **Two audiences can sustain ambiguity.** In Bräuninger and Giger's model, leaders seek both electoral votes and continued party office. Voters have diffuse priors, while decisive party members have concentrated priors near their own preferred policies. The same platform consequently produces different posterior beliefs.
- **Risk aversion does not rule out strategic ambiguity.** At a fixed platform mean, greater variance reduces voter utility. Its benefit to the leader arises from preserving activist support while allowing movement toward the electorate; ambiguity need not be intrinsically attractive to voters.
- **Electoral pressure is party-specific.** The weighted electoral mean weights each voter's ideal point by $\rho_{ij}(1-\rho_{ij})$. It depends on the entire strategy profile, rather than being a fixed electorate-wide median or mean.
- **Position and precision are separate choices.** The model and simulations motivate a U-shaped relationship between platform location and ambiguity: parties near the center face less conflict between principals, while more extreme parties can benefit from broader platforms. This is a conditional prediction, not a definition of ambiguity.
- **Textual dispersion is not estimation error.** A Wordscores-based measure uses the frequency-weighted standard deviation of ideological word scores. Dividing it by the square root of the number of scored words instead produces a standard error of the estimated mean, a different quantity.
- **Communication and reception must be distinguished.** A manifesto's ideological dispersion, silence on a particular issue, inconsistent messages across channels, an individual voter's uncertainty, and disagreement across voters are not interchangeable measures. A single manifesto captures only part of this landscape.
- **Distance uncertainty can be voter-specific.** Martin measures ambiguity as $\operatorname{SD}(\lVert z_v-Z_p\rVert_2)$, where $z_v$ is a voter's position and $Z_p$ is a distribution of respondent-perceived party positions. In multiple dimensions, equally distant voters can face different distance uncertainty because party dispersion varies by direction. Interpreting this as individual uncertainty assumes voters recognize the disagreement represented in that distribution.
- **Proximity gains can coexist with evaluation penalties.** Martin finds ambiguity associated with shorter perceived distance but lower party sympathy conditional on that distance, with a stronger negative association among politically interested voters. These observational patterns suggest competing pathways; they do not identify causal mediation or show that low-interest voters prefer ambiguity.
- **Voter priorities condition the electoral association.** Han's analysis of the economy and immigration finds lower voting propensity associated with ambiguity among voters who prioritize the issue, but no significant benefit among other voters. Expert disagreement can also reflect internal party division or measurement uncertainty, so this evidence does not by itself identify intentional strategy.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/party-ambiguity-and-individual-preferences|Party ambiguity and individual preferences]]: develops a multidimensional voter-party ambiguity measure and relates it to sympathy, perceived distance, and political interest in eight European election surveys.
- [[papers/who-refuses-ambiguity-voters-issue-salience-and-the-electoral-effect-of-party-position-ambiguity|Who refuses ambiguity? Voters' issue salience and the electoral effect of party position ambiguity]]: shows that the negative association between issue-specific expert disagreement and voting propensity is concentrated among voters prioritizing the issue.
- [[papers/strategic-ambiguity-of-party-positions-in-multi-party-competition|Strategic Ambiguity of Party Positions in Multi-Party Competition]]: formalizes the voter-activist trade-off and finds observational support for moderation and a U-shaped ambiguity relationship in European manifesto data. It does not directly identify intentional ambiguity or its causal effect on votes.
