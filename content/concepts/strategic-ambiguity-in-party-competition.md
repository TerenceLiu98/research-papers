## Overview

Strategic ambiguity in party competition is the deliberate choice to leave a party's promised policy position imprecise. It adds the breadth or dispersion of a platform to its location as a strategic choice. Ambiguity can help leaders accommodate audiences with different expectations, but its value depends on how those audiences form beliefs and reward the leader.

## Key Ideas

- **Two audiences can sustain ambiguity.** In Bräuninger and Giger's model, leaders seek both electoral votes and continued party office. Voters have diffuse priors, while decisive party members have concentrated priors near their own preferred policies. The same platform consequently produces different posterior beliefs.
- **Risk aversion does not rule out strategic ambiguity.** At a fixed platform mean, greater variance reduces voter utility. Its benefit to the leader arises from preserving activist support while allowing movement toward the electorate; ambiguity need not be intrinsically attractive to voters.
- **Electoral pressure is party-specific.** The weighted electoral mean weights each voter's ideal point by $\rho_{ij}(1-\rho_{ij})$. It depends on the entire strategy profile, rather than being a fixed electorate-wide median or mean.
- **Position and precision are separate choices.** The model and simulations motivate a U-shaped relationship between platform location and ambiguity: parties near the center face less conflict between principals, while more extreme parties can benefit from broader platforms. This is a conditional prediction, not a definition of ambiguity.
- **Textual dispersion is not estimation error.** A Wordscores-based measure uses the frequency-weighted standard deviation of ideological word scores. Dividing it by the square root of the number of scored words instead produces a standard error of the estimated mean, a different quantity.
- **Communication and reception must be distinguished.** A manifesto's ideological dispersion, silence on a particular issue, inconsistent messages across channels, an individual voter's uncertainty, and disagreement across voters are not interchangeable measures. A single manifesto captures only part of this landscape.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/strategic-ambiguity-of-party-positions-in-multi-party-competition|Strategic Ambiguity of Party Positions in Multi-Party Competition]]: formalizes the voter-activist trade-off and finds observational support for moderation and a U-shaped ambiguity relationship in European manifesto data.
