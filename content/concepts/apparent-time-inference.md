## Overview

Apparent-time inference uses linguistic differences between birth cohorts observed in a survey to reconstruct community change. Its central assumption is that speakers' language becomes sufficiently stable after adolescence for older respondents to retain evidence of earlier community usage. This connects age differences to historical change without requiring a sequence of historical surveys.

## Key Ideas

- **Cohort as a historical proxy.** In the linked dialect study, birth year $t$ represents acquisition during an interval approximately $[t,t+a]$, where $a$ is the age at which language stabilizes. Birth year is not an exact observation date for the community state.
- **Historical change versus individual aging.** Differences across cohorts can also reflect language changes during individual lives. Later-life adoption weakens the interpretation of respondents as records of their adolescent communities; the linked paper argues this can make inferred change rates conservative.
- **Spatial reconstruction requires acquisition location.** Where speakers learned their variants is the relevant geographic information. Smoothing across space and cohorts helps estimate fields from sparse observations, but adds modeling assumptions.
- **Validation has several levels.** Agreement with known dialect boundaries supports spatial plausibility. Direct comparisons with repeated observations are needed to evaluate historical timing and lifespan stability; spatial agreement alone does not settle those issues.
- **Forecasts inherit the measurement process.** Prediction against smoothed apparent-time fields measures agreement with a reconstruction. When that reconstruction uses all cohorts, it is not an independent future-survey test.
