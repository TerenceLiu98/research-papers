## Overview

Firm gerrymandering is the disproportionate allocation of corporate locations to a party's electoral districts through boundary drawing, evaluated against a specified distribution of feasible maps. Firms may be valuable because they provide campaign resources, policy information, visibility, and career opportunities. The concept concerns the political assignment of existing locations, rather than firms physically relocating.

## Key Ideas

- **An additional redistricting objective:** Seat totals do not exhaust the political value of a map. Two plans with the same seat allocation can assign different numbers of firms to each party.
- **Unconditional comparison:** Compare the enacted allocation of firms with its distribution across [[concepts/redistricting-ensembles|Redistricting Ensembles]]. This accounts for modeled geography and constraints, rather than assuming firms should be proportional to statewide votes.
- **Seat-conditional comparison:** Restrict the ensemble to maps with the enacted seat total. An extreme firm allocation within this subset supports targeting beyond seat gains alone.
- **Identification needs variation:** If almost every map with a particular seat allocation also gives the party the same firms, seat and firm targeting are observationally indistinguishable. Extreme unconditional allocations alone do not establish deliberate targeting.
- **Distinguish resources from consequences:** Assigning headquarters to co-partisan districts creates a geographic alignment. Effects on contributions, incumbency, lobbying, or policy require additional evidence.
- **Specify the unit and scope:** Headquarters, establishments, employment, and firm value are different outcomes. Statewide party counts can conceal redistribution between incumbents within a party. Broader economic gerrymandering could involve other assets, but evidence for headquarters does not demonstrate those extensions.
