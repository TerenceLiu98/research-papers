## Overview

The marginal-trader hypothesis holds that a market price can be accurate even when the average participant is biased or demographically unrepresentative. What matters for price formation is the behavior of traders at the financially consequential margin: active participants whose trades respond to prices and information rather than simply expressing preferences.

## Key Ideas

- A prediction-market price is not a survey average. Traders differ in stake size, activity, information, and willingness to trade at a given price.
- Unrepresentative participation alone does not imply biased prices. Selection must also be associated with biased beliefs or trade direction among the capital that carries price-setting weight.
- Financial incentives can discipline repeated traders: systematically preference-driven trades lose money, while informed or arbitrage trades can scale up.
- Empirical tests should weight behavior by financial influence and compare trade direction across activity levels. A bias among numerous low-volume users can coexist with little price effect.
- Two-sided traders may help maintain prices without expressing a directional political belief, so trader counts and net directional exposure answer different questions.
- The hypothesis does not guarantee accuracy. Thin liquidity, binding position limits, correlated errors among active traders, or biased price-setting capital can still produce distorted prices.

## Important Papers

- Forsythe, Nelson, Neumann, and Wright (1992), "Anatomy of an Experimental Political Stock Market," *American Economic Review* 82(5), 1142-1161: introduces the hypothesis to explain accurate Iowa Electronic Market prices from a narrow participant pool.
- Oliven and Rietz (2004), "Suckers Are Born, but Markets Are Made," *Management Science* 50(3), 336-351: examines individual rationality, arbitrage, and market efficiency in an electronic futures market.
- [[papers/diversity-efficiency-and-incentives-in-political-prediction-markets|Diversity, Efficiency, and Incentives in Political Prediction Markets]]: reports that the association between inferred politics and dollar-weighted trade direction declines with activity and is absent among Kalshi traders with more than 100 trades.
