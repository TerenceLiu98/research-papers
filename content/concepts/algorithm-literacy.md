## Overview

Algorithm literacy is the capacity to understand algorithmic systems and evaluate their outputs and implications for judgment. It involves more than basic tool use or programming skill. The tax-administration study distinguishes knowledge of algorithms from reflection on algorithms to examine how officials perceive AI-supported decision-making.

## Key Ideas

- **Knowledge of algorithms (KOA)** concerns awareness and understanding of operational principles, including how systems process data, draw inferences, and generate outputs. Self-reported knowledge should not be equated with demonstrated technical proficiency.
- **Reflection on algorithms (ROA)** concerns evaluation of benefits, limitations, bias, and risks. In the study's conceptualization it includes positive appraisals of decision support as well as critical concerns; it is broader than skepticism alone.
- **Aggregation can hide opposing patterns.** In the tax study, aggregate literacy does not significantly moderate automation's association with [[concepts/street-level-discretion|Street-Level Discretion]], whereas a joint exploratory model yields negative KOA and positive ROA interactions. These are conditional estimates from correlated dimensions, not evidence that literacy is generally harmful or beneficial.
- **Interpretation differs from mechanism identification.** The authors suggest that knowledge tempers inflated expectations while reflection supports selective appropriation of AI outputs. The experiment does not randomize literacy or test a training intervention, so these explanations remain interpretations supported by contextual interviews.
- **Literacy and authority are complementary.** Recognizing a flawed recommendation does not itself grant permission or procedures to override it. [[concepts/human-oversight-of-ai|Human Oversight of AI]] also requires institutional authority and accountability.
