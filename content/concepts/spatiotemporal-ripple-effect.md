## Overview

The Spatiotemporal Ripple Effect describes temporally ordered propagation of an urban development trajectory: growth begins earlier in a core, reaches adjacent transition zones later, and appears in peripheral zones after a longer delay. Unlike contemporaneous spatial association, the concept emphasizes ordering, lag structure, and the possible superposition of multiple historical diffusion impulses.

## Key Ideas

- **Ordered timing:** The defining evidence is a sequence of growth initiation, acceleration, or inflection points from core through transition to periphery, not merely higher values near the center.
- **Layered impulses:** A core may generate several waves with different timing and intensity. Their overlap can make transition-zone trajectories especially heterogeneous and sensitive to partition scale.
- **Pattern-process relation:** The effect operates within a [[concepts/core-transition-periphery-structure|Core-Transition-Periphery Structure]]. The structure channels propagation, while repeated propagation helps reproduce the structure.
- **Distinction from spillovers:** Standard spatial spillover measures often emphasize contemporaneous dependence. A ripple claim requires evidence about long-run temporal sequencing among spatial zones.
- **Distinction from growth-pole diffusion:** Growth-pole theory broadly predicts outward influence; the ripple formulation focuses on trajectory-level phase differences and historically layered transmission at the intra-urban scale.
- **Causal caution:** Lagged and spatially ordered curves are compatible with diffusion but do not alone identify a causal transmission mechanism or rule out common policy, infrastructure, or measurement processes.
