## Overview

Cognitive offloading delegates cognitive operations to external tools or representations. Writing, information resources, and LLMs can reduce immediate cognitive demands, but the benefit during use and the effect on later independent performance are separate questions. Offloading can support learning and expanded capability or displace the practice that maintains a skill.

## Key Ideas

- **Scaffolding versus substitution.** Scaffolding preserves or develops the user's subsequent ability to perform a task. Substitution performs the underlying operation for the user and can remove opportunities to exercise it. The same tool can support either pattern depending on task design and use.
- **Assisted performance versus unaided competence.** High-quality output with a tool does not establish what a person can do after the tool is withdrawn. Conversely, external support does not by itself establish skill loss.
- **Use versus dependence.** In [[papers/large-language-models-as-a-cognitive-virus|Large-Language Models as a Cognitive Virus]], autonomous regular use and persistent substitutive dependence are separate model states. Their proportions depend on progression and recovery rates, so widespread adoption need not mean widespread dependence.
- **Social maintenance of autonomy.** The same paper assumes that institutions and peers reinforce independent reasoning more effectively when such practices are common. This can generate [[concepts/bistability-and-hysteresis|Bistability and Hysteresis]], but is a theoretical mechanism requiring empirical evaluation.
- **Preserving active cognition.** Verification, reconstruction of arguments, and recurring unaided practice are proposed ways to keep support complementary. The population model does not test their effectiveness.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/large-language-models-as-a-cognitive-virus|Large-Language Models as a Cognitive Virus]]: connects offloading and dependence to population dynamics while treating competence weights as illustrative assumptions.
