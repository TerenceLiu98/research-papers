## Overview

Right-wing populist mobilisation brings people into collective political activity around claims combining opposition between the people and elites with exclusionary ideas about national belonging. Electoral support and street protest are distinct expressions that may cluster in the same places; explaining their relationship requires evidence about local conditions, organisations, and participants.

## Key Ideas

- **Economic grievances:** Perceived exclusion or fear of decline may make mobilisation attractive. Average local income does not directly measure individual insecurity or inequality, so a positive city-income association cannot by itself confirm or refute an individual grievance mechanism.
- **Cultural grievances and contact:** Nativist mobilisation draws on boundaries between natives and migrants. Intergroup contact is a proposed countervailing mechanism, but foreign-born population share is a contextual indicator rather than direct evidence of contact quality or attitude change.
- **Ideological foundations:** Prior right-wing electoral support may indicate a receptive local environment. Its association with later protest does not establish that the same individuals voted and demonstrated.
- **Place and timing:** Relatively stable local conditions may help explain where a movement spreads, while salient events may coincide with mobilisation waves. These are different explanatory tasks and require different evidence.
- **Organisational boundaries:** Movement actors, parties, and extremist groups can appear at the same protests. Event-level actor records document reported participation; stronger claims about coordination or shared membership require additional evidence.
