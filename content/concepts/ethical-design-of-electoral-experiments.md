## Overview

Electoral experiments can affect officeholders and communities beyond enrolled subjects. An explicit objective, such as avoiding changes in who wins, can be translated into restrictions on where and how densely an intervention is randomized.

## Key Ideas

- Specify the counterfactual: no intervention for a researcher-created treatment, or the allocation a partner would otherwise implement. Randomization can matter by withholding as well as adding treatment.
- Aggregate exposure at the electoral district level. A study's sample size alone does not reveal its potential to change a race.
- Bound possible individual responses rather than assuming a small average effect implies negligible electoral risk. Interference expands the set of voters whose responses must be considered.
- Compare the impact bound with uncertainty about the margin needed to change officeholders. In Slough's framework, a two-sided vote transfer motivates comparing twice maximum aggregate electoral impact with a lower predictive quantile of the margin.
- Ethical constraints can restrict power and generalization: predictable, large districts may support interventions that close, small, or poorly forecast races cannot.
- This criterion addresses a specified aggregate outcome; it does not exhaust research ethics or establish that every design passing it is ethically acceptable.
