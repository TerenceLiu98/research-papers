## Overview

Structural risk describes conditions that make a political relationship vulnerable to conflict, such as geographic proximity, capabilities, alliances, and regime characteristics. Conflict triggers are proximate events or idiosyncratic developments that may precipitate escalation within those conditions. The distinction separates susceptibility to war from information about its timing.

## Key Ideas

- **Conditional vulnerability:** In the stable-equilibrium analogy, disturbances dissipate; in a vulnerable equilibrium, a small disturbance can initiate escalation. High structural risk can coexist with continued peace when no consequential disturbance occurs.
- **Broad trigger definition:** A trigger need not be a single dramatic incident. Context, leadership, and changing perceptions can matter, and the same recorded event can have different implications in different settings.
- **Indirect measurement:** Contemporary news and financial prices can summarize observers' interpretations of developments. They also reflect underlying conditions, so they are imperfect and overlapping proxies rather than clean measurements of independent shocks.
- **Levels and changes differ:** A persistently high signal and a sudden increase carry different information. Chadefaux's models include both levels and monthly differences; their coefficients need not have the same sign or significance.
- **Forecasting and explanation:** Held-out predictive improvement demonstrates information beyond a chosen baseline. It does not establish that the signal causes conflict, that its source is random, or that better structural predictors cannot account for it.
- **Joint risk is not automatically interaction evidence:** An additive logistic model can predict high probabilities when both inputs are large. A causal claim that structural vulnerability amplifies a trigger requires evidence beyond that joint prediction.
