## Overview

Moral Foundations Theory describes moral judgment as drawing on multiple partially distinct foundations. The commonly used five-foundation formulation distinguishes care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation. In political communication research, foundation language can be measured in messages, linked to political identity, or used to study how audiences respond to rhetoric.

## Key Ideas

- Care/harm emphasizes protecting others from suffering; fairness/cheating concerns deservingness and reciprocal treatment; loyalty/betrayal concerns group membership; authority/subversion concerns order and legitimate authority; and sanctity/degradation concerns purity and contamination.
- Foundations are analytic categories for moral language and judgment, not direct measurements of a speaker's stable moral character or political ideology.
- Text-based measurement requires a transparent dictionary, language adaptation, and validation. Word embeddings can help identify candidate terms, but manual review and contextual checks remain necessary.
- Moral rhetoric can be emotionally intense and can co-occur with insults or other toxic language. Co-occurrence does not by itself establish that moral language causes toxicity.
- The association between a foundation and toxicity can depend on the audience, political setting, outlet, and communication mode. In the Taiwanese comparison by Hsiao and colleagues, loyalty and authority language were especially associated with toxicity in YouTube talk shows.
