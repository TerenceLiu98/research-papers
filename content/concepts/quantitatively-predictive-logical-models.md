## Overview

Quantitatively predictive logical models derive a specific functional relationship from the admissible ranges of variables, necessary anchor points, asymptotic behavior, and minimal substantive premises. Their goal is to predict how much an outcome should change, not merely the direction of association, while avoiding impossible predictions at extreme inputs.

## Key Ideas

- Variable bounds constrain functional form. A model should remain logically possible across the full input domain rather than only near the observed sample mean.
- Anchor points are input values that force a unique outcome. For example, a party system with one party must have zero dispersion among party positions.
- Asymptotes encode limiting expectations when inputs become very large or small. They can turn a directional claim into a quantitatively testable curve.
- Dimensional consistency and scale matter. Standardization can supply a meaningful reference asymptote when the original measurement units have no natural zero or common range.
- Functional forms should follow the constraints. Saturating processes may require nonlinear models even when a linear approximation fits a narrow part of the observed data.
- Separate equations can form an interlocking network. A model connecting electoral institutions to the effective number of parties can be combined with another connecting party numbers to polarization, allowing indirect institutional predictions.
- Empirical estimates test whether theoretically chosen coefficients and shapes are plausible. A low $R^2$ does not by itself refute the predicted mean relationship, but large residual variation limits case-level prediction.
- Logical derivation does not replace causal explanation. Multiple behavioral mechanisms may produce the same constrained functional form, and associational corroboration does not identify which mechanism operates.
