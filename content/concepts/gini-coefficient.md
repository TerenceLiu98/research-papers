## Overview

The Gini coefficient summarizes how unequally a quantity is distributed across units. In policy-change research, applying it to absolute changes measures whether a few time units account for most observed change.

## Key Ideas

- The Lorenz curve orders units by magnitude and plots cumulative quantity shares against cumulative unit shares. Gini equals one minus twice the area under that curve.
- Equal allocation gives zero; greater concentration moves the measure toward one. A completely zero quantity requires an explicit convention because cumulative quantity shares are undefined.
- For policy changes, using absolute values retains magnitude but removes direction. Sorting also removes temporal ordering, so concentration and sequential dynamics are different properties.
- Sampling variability still matters. Better precision under one simulated distribution does not imply universal superiority over other statistics.
