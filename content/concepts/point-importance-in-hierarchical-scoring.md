## Overview

A point’s importance is the difference between the probability of winning the match after winning that point and the probability after losing it. In systems that aggregate points into games and sets, points contribute unequally to the final outcome.

## Key Ideas

- A point that can close a scoring unit is a useful discrete proxy for importance, but its exact influence still depends on the score.
- Winning more points need not imply winning the match: points concentrated in already lopsided sets can have little effect on match outcomes.
- Small, uniform point advantages are amplified by aggregation. In the six formats studied by Martín and colleagues, amplification near equal ability is approximately proportional to the square root of mean match length.
- A player can win most matches with fewer than half the pooled points when crucial points carry disproportionately large influence and the player performs better on them. This region is narrow in the models studied.
- Empirical pressure comparisons must adjust for the serve–return mix. Conditioning on match outcomes also creates mechanical associations, motivating measurement in separate matches or seasons.

## Important Papers

[[papers/beyond-the-federer-paradox-in-tennis|Beyond the Federer paradox in tennis]] links this importance-based condition to exact scoring models and ATP evidence. It credits Morris (1977) with the underlying definition of point importance.

## Related Concepts

Match conversion measures wins relative to a benchmark conditioned on each match’s point share. It differs from pressure performance: tiebreak results and the distribution of margins across sets also affect conversion.
