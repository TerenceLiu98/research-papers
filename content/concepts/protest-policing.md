## Overview

Protest policing comprises law-enforcement decisions about presence, visibility, arrests, equipment, crowd control, and engagement at collective-action events. These decisions can affect participants directly while also signaling to outside observers what kind of event they are seeing. A conspicuous armed deployment may therefore shape perceived protester threat even when the depicted protest behavior is held constant.

## Key Ideas

- **Police response is both an outcome and a potential cause.** Protest tactics, size, timing, and composition may influence deployment, while deployment can alter participant behavior, media coverage, and audience judgments. Designs must specify which direction they estimate.
- **Visible force is an informational cue.** Observers may infer that authorities have reasons for arriving in large numbers or riot gear. That inference can attach perceived violence to protesters without direct evidence of violent tactics.
- **Actual and perceived nonviolence differ.** A movement's strategic benefits depend partly on audience judgments. Nonviolent conduct does not guarantee that observers will perceive an event as nonviolent when police imagery or other contextual cues imply danger.
- **Immediate perceptions and movement support are separate outcomes.** English, White, and Eckhouse find that one heavy-police cue changes judgments of violence but not support or participation intentions. A cumulative pathway from repeated cues to movement-level attitudes remains an interpretation to test.
- **Discretion is not identified by unexplained variance alone.** Measured event characteristics leave substantial variation in police responses unexplained, but omitted circumstances, reporting error, and within-event feedback also contribute. Residual variation is not a direct causal measure of strategic police choice.
- **Audience response can be heterogeneous.** Prior experiences with and trust in police may change how people interpret deployment cues. Exploratory racial differences require adequately powered, preregistered tests and direct measures of the proposed moderators.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/how-police-behavior-shapes-perceptions-of-protests-evidence-from-black-lives-matter|How Police Behavior Shapes Perceptions of Protests: Evidence from Black Lives Matter]]: combines an original dataset of 2014-2017 BLM events with a randomized news-vignette experiment showing that heavy police presence increases perceived protest violence but does not significantly reduce protest support.
- Earl and Soule (2006), "Seeing Blue: A Police-Centered Explanation of Protest Policing": cited foundation for explaining variation in police presence at protest events.
- Della Porta and Reiter (1998), *Policing Protest: The Control of Mass Demonstrations in Western Democracies*: cited comparative framework for protest policing.
