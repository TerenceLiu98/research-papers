## Overview

A situation in which measures taken by a state to improve its security reduce the security of other states, potentially generating reciprocal competition.

## Key Ideas

The dilemma can arise among states seeking security without expansionist aims. Distinguishing offensive from defensive postures affects whether military preparations communicate threatening intent.

Beliefs about the speed and cost of war shape incentives for arms racing and preemption. Nilsson argues that recognizing the possibility of prolonged war can alleviate the dilemma relative to accounts that assume rapid victory under offense dominance. This is a theoretical implication of duration evidence, not a direct empirical test of security competition.

Corneo’s model of NATO enlargement shows how pooling can obscure motives: both revisionist and security-seeking states may choose enlargement or preventive attack. Observing these actions then fails to identify a state’s type; this is an equilibrium possibility, not evidence that the historical actors were necessarily security-seekers.
