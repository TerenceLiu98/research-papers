## Overview

Geographic polarization is increasing separation of political support across places, such as growing Democratic strength in urban areas alongside growing Republican strength in rural areas. It is one spatial manifestation of [[concepts/political-polarization|Political Polarization]], distinct from a direct measure of ideological distance or hostility between individual voters.

## Key Ideas

- **District boundaries affect measurement:** An enacted district's partisan composition reflects both where voters live and how boundaries group them. [[concepts/redistricting-ensembles|Redistricting Ensembles]] compare patterns across alternative maps to reduce dependence on particular enacted boundaries.
- **Polarization and bias differ:** Opposing regional shifts can intensify geographic separation while largely canceling in national expected seat totals. Stronger urban-rural polarization need not increase one party's aggregate advantage.
- **Competition can decline without a growing net bias:** When already partisan areas become more homogeneous, fewer districts may contain balanced partisan populations, even under nonpartisan map generation.
- **Keep electoral environments comparable:** A fixed national vote baseline helps distinguish changing local patterns from nationwide partisan swings. Estimated geographic seat advantages can still vary with the chosen baseline.
- **Geographic change is not a migration estimate:** Changes in local voting patterns and population do not identify residential sorting, persuasion, cohort replacement, or other mechanisms separately.
- **Measurement is scale-specific:** In Jasny et al. (2025), urbanity is the share of district voters in urban census blocks using a common 2020 classification. A relatively rural district within the national distribution need not have a rural majority.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/gerrymandering-and-geographic-polarization-have-reduced-electoral-competition|Gerrymandering and geographic polarization have reduced electoral competition]]: finds greater urban-rural separation and a decline in simulated competitive congressional districts from about 67 to 50 between the 2010 and 2020 cycles.
- Chen and Rodden (2013), "Unintentional Gerrymandering: Political Geography and Electoral Bias in Legislatures": cited in the ingested paper as background on geographic sources of seat bias.
- Brown and Mettler (2024), "Sequential Polarization: The Development of the Rural-Urban Political Divide, 1976-2020": cited in the ingested paper as background on the development of the urban-rural divide.
