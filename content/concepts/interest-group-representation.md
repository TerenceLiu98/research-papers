## Overview

Interest group representation is the transmission of social preferences and interests into public decision-making through organized associations rather than electoral office. Groups aggregate demands, frame issues, provide information, mobilize supporters, and lobby officials, but their contribution to democratic congruence depends on which preferences mobilize and how much political weight different groups exercise.

## Key Ideas

- Descriptive approaches infer representation from group type, such as business associations, trade unions, or public-interest organizations. Substantive approaches instead compare the positions advanced by groups with citizen preferences or policy outcomes.
- Interest groups lack parties' formal role in electoral delegation, but they face lower barriers to organizational entry and can advocate narrower combinations of positions. They may therefore occupy parts of a multidimensional issue space that parties leave vacant.
- Coverage is not influence. Counting groups can overstate weak or inactive voices, so analyses may incorporate lobbying frequency, resources, access, or policy effects while recognizing that each is an incomplete measure of political weight.
- Organized interests can improve congruence by amplifying underserved preferences or worsen it by reinforcing existing biases. Collective-action barriers, unequal resources, selective access, and nonresponse make compensation contingent rather than automatic.
- In [[Making space: citizens, parties and interest groups in two ideological dimensions]], activity-weighted group positions point toward party-system gaps in three of five countries, while pointing elsewhere in Belgium and Slovenia. The study demonstrates representational potential, not realized policy influence.
