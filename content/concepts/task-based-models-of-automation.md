## Overview

Task-based models represent production as a collection of activities allocated among labor, capital, or different worker types. Automation changes the feasible allocation by allowing capital to perform tasks previously assigned to labor, while the creation of new tasks can expand the range in which labor has a comparative advantage. This approach makes employment and factor shares depend on which factor performs which tasks, not only on an aggregate elasticity between capital and labor.

## Key Ideas

- **Technology and prices jointly allocate tasks.** Making a task technically automatable does not ensure that capital performs it; firms compare the rental rate of capital with task-specific effective labor costs.
- **Automation has displacement and productivity effects.** Reassigning tasks to capital directly reduces labor demand, while any resulting productivity gain can raise wages and output. The net wage effect can differ between the short run and the long run as capital adjusts.
- **New tasks reinstate labor.** New, more complex activities can counter displacement when workers have a comparative advantage in them. The balance between displaced and reinstated tasks governs employment and labor's income share.
- **Task boundaries transmit distributional change.** Automation concentrated in tasks performed by lower-skill workers and new tasks initially suited to higher-skill workers can both widen skill inequality.
- **The source of an automation surge matters.** A temporary cluster of automation inventions can trigger compensating task creation, whereas a permanent shift in the innovation frontier can lower the balanced-growth employment level and labor share.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/the-race-between-man-and-machine-implications-of-technology-for-growth-factor-shares-and-employment|The Race between Man and Machine: Implications of Technology for Growth, Factor Shares, and Employment]]: integrates automation and new-task creation with capital accumulation and endogenous innovation.
- Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003), "The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change": links computerization to changes in routine and nonroutine task demand.
- Acemoglu and Autor (2011), "Skills, Tasks and Technologies: Implications for Employment and Earnings": develops a task-based framework for skills, technologies, and earnings.
- Zeira (1998), "Workers, Machines, and Economic Growth": models mechanization as adoption of capital-intensive production technologies.
