## Overview

Factional competition is rivalry among organized groups within a political party for offices, resources, or policy influence. It can motivate contributions to collective electoral success or divert effort toward undermining internal rivals.

## Key Ideas

- **Separate collective success from internal rank.** A faction can benefit from a larger party reward pool and from obtaining a greater share of that pool. These incentives need not point toward the same activities.
- **Electoral stakes change cooperation incentives.** In Invernizzi's model, greater rewards to the winning party and greater policy costs of defeat encourage campaigning. Sharing more power with losing parties makes internal rivalry relatively attractive.
- **Reward the activity measured by the contest.** Strong rank-based rewards promote campaigning only when the ranking technology favors campaigning over sabotage. Equal office allocations can discourage destructive competition when rank primarily rewards sabotage.
- **Distinguish levels of ideological comparison.** Under the model's relevant conditions, factions in a moderate party campaign more than those in an extreme party, yet the extreme faction within a divided party campaigns more than its moderate partner.
- **Policy influence differs from office rewards.** Victory-contingent policy concessions can motivate otherwise less committed factions even where rank-based office rewards would encourage sabotage.

These are conditional incentive mechanisms, not a claim that all factional rivalry is destructive or that electoral rules mechanically determine party behavior.
