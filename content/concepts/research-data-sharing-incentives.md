## Overview

Research data sharing incentives attach benefits, such as favorable grant evaluation, to making research data available. Their effects depend on whether those benefits offset the resources and effort needed to prepare data and whether researchers expect sharing to improve future outcomes. Incentives, enforceable mandates, and infrastructure support are distinct policy mechanisms.

## Key Ideas

- **Reward strength and funding coverage interact.** Increasing the weight of sharing in grant evaluation differs from increasing the proportion of teams funded. A large reward that few teams receive may not sustain widespread sharing.
- **Adoption and persistence are separate outcomes.** A funding scheme can produce an early surge followed by decline as teams learn whether costly sharing pays off.
- **Social influence depends on the initial norm.** In the Klebel et al. model, a low-sharing neighborhood depresses sharing effort. This is a consequence of its specified conformity rule, not a universal effect of scientific collaboration.
- **Responses can be non-monotonic.** In some modeled networks, intermediate incentives sustain more sharing than stronger incentives, and very broad grant coverage weakens the incentive to share.
- **Equity has multiple dimensions.** Reduced dependence on initial resources can coexist with persistence of later funding success. Sharing rates and resource inequality should be assessed separately.
- **Compliance is not data quality.** A higher frequency of sharing does not by itself establish reusable data, improved reproducibility, or a durable commitment to openness. Infrastructure and training are proposed complements, but their effects are not tested in the focal model.
