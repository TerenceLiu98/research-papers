## Overview

Modes of future generation classify how actors produce expectations about the future, rather than only what outcomes they imagine. As adapted from Graf and Herzog in Sevenard and Cook's analysis, the framework distinguishes expectation, creation, risk, and conservation. Multiple modes can coexist within the same text or political project.

## Key Ideas

| Mode | How the future is generated | Indicative textual evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expectation | Projects trends or external forces as arriving independently of discretionary intervention | Historical laws, inevitability, momentum, or tides of history |
| Creation | Specifies a future to be actively built through decisions and planning | Blueprints, deadlines, obligations, programs, and institutional commitments |
| Risk | Constructs threatening scenarios to motivate prevention | Dangerous turning points, impending breakdown, or existential threats |
| Conservation | Seeks to carry valued features of the present into the future | Preservation of sovereignty, diversity, institutions, or ecological conditions |

Expectation aligns with passive timing, whereas creation emphasizes active timing. Risk here names a way of generating preventive scenarios; it need not imply a quantified probability. Conservation identifies what is to be preserved, and can therefore coexist with proposals for substantial institutional change.

Timing indexicals connect these modes to observable language, but isolated keywords do not establish a passage's function. Context determines whether a claim projects an inevitable trend, demands intervention, warns against loss, or preserves a valued arrangement. Sevenard and Cook combine the modes into [[superposed-present-futures|Superposed Present Futures]]: expectation and creation support inevitability, while risk and conservation support fragility. That pairing is their analytical application rather than a requirement that all cases follow it.
