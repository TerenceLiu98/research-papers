## Overview

Conspiracy entrepreneurship is the construction and commercial exploitation of an opportunity through an evolving conspiratorial counternarrative about a negative public event. In the model developed by Chase, Maitlis, and Shepherd, prolonging the audience's effort to interpret the event helps preserve that opportunity. The concept concerns the organization and monetization of engagement, rather than conspiracy belief alone.

## Key Ideas

- **Ambiguity as a continuing resource:** Unresolved questions can keep an audience participating even without convergence on one explanation. [[Perpetuated Sensegiving]] describes how interpretation is repeatedly redirected and reopened.
- **A credible participatory arena:** Professional media conventions, invited guests, call-ins, and opportunities to contribute observations can make a platform appear authoritative while giving participants a sense of agency.
- **Different treatment of event and venture:** The entrepreneur sustains doubt about the event while presenting the venture and its commercial offerings as legitimate and understandable.
- **Consumption as participation:** Products can be framed as funding investigation, preparing for threats, or affirming membership in a community of purported truth-seekers. These are interpretations advanced by the case study, not universal consumer motives.
- **Commercial integration:** Advertising can follow unresolved revelations, and programming can respond to observed engagement and sales spikes. A reinforcing revenue-to-sensegiving mechanism is proposed but was not directly examined in the originating study.
- **Scope and evidence:** The concept was developed from the Alex Jones/Sandy Hook case. The proposed conditions include institutional distrust, polarization, and identity value from continued inquiry. Observable practices do not by themselves establish deliberate intent, and generalization requires further cases.

## Important Papers

- [[Ambiguity as Opportunity: A Theoretical Model of Conspiracy Entrepreneurship]]: Introduces the model through inductive analysis of court records, broadcasts, and secondary archives; accepted authors' version, forthcoming in *Academy of Management Journal*.
- Lounsbury and Glynn (2001), "Cultural entrepreneurship: Stories, legitimacy, and the acquisition of resources": A theoretical foundation discussed by the originating paper for understanding how narratives mobilize resources.
