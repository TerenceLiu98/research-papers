## Overview

Bridging social capital consists of ties and norms of trust and reciprocity that connect people across social groups. It concerns cooperation extending beyond close associates, in contrast to bonding social capital within families, communities, or other cohesive groups. Strong internal solidarity can therefore coexist with weak intergroup trust.

## Key Ideas

- Cross-group connections provide opportunities for generalized trust, but contact frequency alone does not establish cooperative relationships.
- [[concepts/diffuse-reciprocity|Diffuse Reciprocity]] offers one mechanism by which a positive cross-group encounter can affect behavior toward other out-group members.
- Program participants can transmit cooperative behavior to their wider networks, but those networks can also pull participants back toward conflictual norms.
- In the Peterson, Galesic, and Hammond model, behavioral change and additional contact among participants complement each other. This is a conditional simulation result, not evidence that any increase in intergroup contact builds trust.
- Cooperation rates can operationalize one aspect of social capital in a model; they do not measure the full empirical concept of trust, ties, and norms.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/a-model-of-long-term-conflict-resolution-and-cooperation|A Model of Long-Term Conflict Resolution and Cooperation]]: models the spread and erosion of intergroup cooperation after small interventions.
- Putnam (2000), *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community*: the source paper's foundation for the bonding/bridging distinction.
- Granovetter (1973), "The Strength of Weak Ties": cited by the source paper to motivate the significance of connections beyond close social circles.
