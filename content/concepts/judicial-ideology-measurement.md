## Overview

Judicial ideology measurement estimates latent orientations toward law and courts. Different sources observe different manifestations or correlates of those orientations, so appointing-party affiliation, voting positions, and practitioner judgments need not be interchangeable.

## Key Ideas

- Vote-based measures infer preferences from case outcomes or patterns of agreement. Political proxies transfer information from appointments, campaign contributions, or associated actors. Third-party measures use observers’ assessments of judging.
- Expert descriptions can capture reasoning and courtroom behavior beyond votes. Their validity depends on evaluator familiarity, sampling, coding, and comparable interpretations across judges.
- Repeated assessments permit change over time, but changing meanings of ideological labels complicate comparisons across distant periods.
- Predicting judicial votes provides an external behavioral check beyond correlations between measures. Different coverage and outcome samples must remain visible in comparisons.
- An aggregate left-right position can hide issue-specific or cross-cutting orientations; disagreement among evaluations can reflect substantive complexity as well as noise.
