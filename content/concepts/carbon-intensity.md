## Overview

Carbon intensity measures carbon dioxide emissions relative to economic output, commonly as CO2 per unit of GDP. It distinguishes the emissions efficiency of economic activity from total emissions: intensity can decline while aggregate emissions rise if output grows sufficiently quickly. Because it combines an emissions numerator with an economic denominator, changes can reflect energy efficiency, fuel mix, industrial structure, output growth, or measurement choices.

## Key Ideas

- **It is a ratio, not an emissions total.** Lower carbon intensity means less measured CO2 per unit of output, but does not necessarily mean lower total CO2 emissions.
- **Both numerator and denominator matter.** Energy use and carbon coefficients shape estimated emissions, while GDP definitions, prices, and geographic accounting shape measured output.
- **Structural change can alter the metric.** A shift from heavy industry toward services may reduce territorial intensity even when consumption drives emissions elsewhere.
- **Targets and causal effects differ.** Meeting an intensity target after a policy begins does not establish that the policy caused the improvement. Evaluation requires a credible counterfactual for what intensity would have been without the policy.
- **Time horizon matters.** Construction of low-carbon infrastructure may raise emissions before operational benefits appear, so short- and long-run policy effects can differ.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/does-low-carbon-pilot-city-program-reduce-carbon-intensity|Does Low-Carbon Pilot City Program Reduce Carbon Intensity? Evidence from Chinese Cities]] (Feng et al., 2021): estimates that China's pilot program slowed city-level intensity declines relative to comparison cities during 2005-2018.
- Narayan and Narayan (2010), "Carbon Dioxide Emissions and Economic Growth: Panel Data Evidence from Developing Countries": examines the relationship between emissions and growth in developing economies.
- Shan et al. (2018), "China CO2 Emission Accounts 1997-2015": develops emissions accounts used in subsequent Chinese carbon-intensity research.
