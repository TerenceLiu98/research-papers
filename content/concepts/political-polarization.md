## Overview

Political polarization is the differentiation and alignment of political actors into increasingly opposed positions or groups. It can describe ideological distance, partisan sorting, negative affect toward an opposing group, or segregation in communication networks. A complex-systems perspective treats these not only as individual attributes but as emergent outcomes of interactions among citizens, elites, parties, media, institutions, and social networks.

## Key Ideas

- Polarization has multiple dimensions. Ideological separation, affective hostility, party sorting, issue alignment, and network segregation require different measures and need not change together.
- Some disagreement can clarify alternatives and preserve political diversity. Extreme polarization becomes dangerous when it collapses a multidimensional issue space into a small number of aligned camps or excludes viable responses.
- Local mechanisms can generate system-level patterns. Homophily, attraction and repulsion, partisan learning, information cascades, link recommendation, and elite-public feedback can each alter collective trajectories.
- Feedback creates nonlinearity. Polarization may accelerate after a threshold, become asymmetric, display hysteresis, or resist reversal by shocks that affect all groups.
- Network structure changes what actors can learn about one another. Diverse preferences may support productive coordination, while segregation can hide those preferences and obstruct collective action.
- Consequences extend beyond political competition. Polarization can narrow the set of accepted public goods and weaken cooperation over their provision at local, national, and international scales.
- Institutions and interventions are mechanism-dependent. Greater cross-cutting exposure, stakeholder participation, redistribution, or institutional reform can moderate polarization under some models, but effects depend on prior attitudes, network topology, and governance context.
