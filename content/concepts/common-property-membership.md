## Overview

Common-property membership defines who may use collectively owned resources and participate in their governance. It differs from open access: benefits are reserved for a bounded group, even when the resources are jointly managed within that group. Membership can function as a non-transferable asset when it is inherited, cannot be privately sold, and yields benefits only while its holder resides in the community.

## Key Ideas

- **Access and ownership are distinct.** Collective ownership does not imply that every resident or outsider may use the resource; membership supplies the exclusion boundary.
- **Household-level benefits.** When one spouse's membership grants an entire household access, marriage becomes a route for migration into the commons.
- **Inheritance rules shape mobility.** Egalitarian transmission allows either spouse to carry membership to descendants. Patrilineal or matrilineal transmission narrows that route and can alter residence and marriage choices.
- **Commons and private property can diverge.** A legal system may preserve equal inheritance of private assets while restricting the inheritance of collective membership.
- **Congestion creates political incentives.** Where benefits are limited and allocated per household, immigration can reduce incumbent members' shares and encourage tighter admission rules.
- **Network effects matter.** Closure in one community can redirect migration toward other open communities, linking local membership policy to regional institutional change.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/from-open-to-closed-societies-inequality-migration-and-womens-rights|From Open to Closed Societies: Inequality, Migration, and Women's Rights]]: links unequal commons wealth and marriage migration to the spread of patrilineal membership rules in historical Trentino.
- Casari and Lisciandra (2016), "Gender Discrimination in Property Rights: Six Centuries of Commons Governance in the Alps": documents the long-run change in women's access to Alpine commons.
- Ostrom (1990), "Governing the Commons": establishes a foundational account of institutions for collectively managed resources.
