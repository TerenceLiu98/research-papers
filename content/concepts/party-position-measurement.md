## Overview

Party-position measurement assigns political parties locations on ideological or policy dimensions using evidence such as manifestos, legislative votes, public perceptions, and expert assessments. Those locations support comparisons of parties, coalitions, and electoral systems, but their interpretation depends on what each source observes and which actors it covers.

## Key Ideas

- Manifestos capture stated programs, roll calls capture legislative behavior, public surveys capture perceived locations, and experts supply informed judgments. Combining sources requires attention to these differences rather than assuming they measure identical quantities.
- Coverage changes the system being described. Legislative measures omit actors without seats, while surveys and text collections may miss small parties, independents, or short-lived electoral lists.
- Coalition membership can inform missing positions, but inherited coalition scores and fixed dispersion ranges impose substantive assumptions. Assigning unknown independents to the midpoint is an imputation rule, not evidence of ideological moderation.
- Synthetic perturbations can test sensitivity to assumed measurement variation. Agreement between synthetic and original scores establishes internal stability under the chosen perturbations; external validity requires additional evidence.
- Longitudinal changes in score distributions may reflect party entry, exit, coalition changes, or movement by continuing actors. A system-level trend does not automatically isolate repositioning by individual parties.
- [[concepts/party-system-polarization|Party-System Polarization]] depends on both measured positions and the weights assigned to actors. Equal weighting of electoral lists and weighting by vote shares answer different questions.
