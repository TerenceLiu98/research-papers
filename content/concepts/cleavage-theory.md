## Overview

Cleavage theory links long-term social transformations to enduring political conflict. Structural differences supply potential constituencies, but political mobilisation helps turn them into groups with shared identities, interests, and organisational expression. A demographic voting correlation alone does not establish a cleavage.

## Key Ideas

- Structure, collective identity, and political organisation must be considered together. Their labels need not coincide: educational divisions can be mobilised through national, place-based, or lifestyle identities.
- Contemporary knowledge societies may sustain boundaries through educational networks and spatial or occupational segregation even as formal membership organisations decline.
- A cleavage pole can have multiple party representatives. Switching within a bloc may coexist with relatively stable boundaries between opposing constituencies.
- Historical cleavages were also internally heterogeneous; a uniformly organised working class is an overly demanding benchmark for contemporary cases.
- A cleavage can shape important conflicts without explaining the entire issue space or binding a majority of voters. Whether contemporary cultural conflicts form one cleavage or several remains debated.
