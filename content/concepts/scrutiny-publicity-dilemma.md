## Overview

The scrutiny-publicity dilemma describes the tension facing authoritarian hosts of highly visible international events: publicity can improve their reputation, but the journalists providing it can also expose abuses and amplify opposition claims. Scharpf, Gläßel, and Edwards use this mechanism to explain strategic adjustments in repression around Argentina's 1978 World Cup.

## Key Ideas

- **Attention brings benefits and risks.** A successful event can support domestic legitimacy and international prestige. Independent foreign reporting can instead reveal abuses and undermine the image the government seeks to project.
- **Opposition actors also respond to visibility.** Journalists can provide an audience for dissident claims, increasing the government's perceived need to prevent public challenges during the event.
- **The mechanism predicts timing and location.** [[concepts/preemptive-repression|Preemptive Repression]] should rise before heightened scrutiny in exposed locations; visible violence should fall there while journalists are present. Nonhost areas need not follow the same cycle.
- **Physical presence is different from available attention.** Hotels approximate where journalists stay, while broadcasting hours approximate when they are occupied. The World Cup study reports patterns consistent with both dimensions, without directly observing individual attention.
- **A quieter event does not establish liberalization.** Coercion can move to earlier or later periods or become less visible. Reputational improvement and protection of human rights are separate outcomes.
- **Scope conditions affect predictions.** Regime institutions, digital communication, surveillance, and resources for separating venues from local populations may change how much coercion is used, where it occurs, and how early it begins.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/international-sports-events-and-repression-in-autocracies-evidence-from-the-1978-fifa-world-cup|International Sports Events and Repression in Autocracies: Evidence from the 1978 FIFA World Cup]]: develops the dilemma and examines event timing, journalist hotels, repression types, and broadcasting hours in Argentina. Its extensions to trade fairs, summits, and state visits are proposed applications, not established results.
- DeMeritt (2012), "International Organizations and Government Killing: Does Naming and Shaming Save Lives?": cited by the World Cup study as background for the costs of exposure and international condemnation.
