## Overview

Intellectual humility involves recognizing the fallibility of one's beliefs and the intellectual strengths of others. In communication research it can be operationalized through respect for other views, consideration of counterarguments, and acknowledgment of uncertainty or personal limitations. A person's disposition toward humility and the humility expressed in a particular message are different objects of study.

## Key Ideas

- **Separate traits from wording.** Associations between an author's humility and political behavior do not by themselves identify the effect of changing a message's language. A linguistic intervention can target expressed humility without establishing a change in the author's underlying disposition.
- **Expressions combine multiple dimensions.** Respect, counterargument recognition, and uncertainty need not vary independently. A study should make its operational definition explicit rather than assume that all markers of humility are interchangeable.
- **Other message features can confound comparisons.** Naturally humble and non-humble texts may differ in topic or vividness. Editing a common original aims to isolate humility, conditional on preserving other outcome-relevant content.
- **Perceived civility and persuasiveness can diverge.** In Tierney et al.'s political-message experiment, humble wording reduced perceived aggression by 0.57 points, informativeness by 0.17, and persuasiveness to the reader by 0.16 on five-point scales. These results concern this experiment, not a universal tradeoff.
- **Message ratings are limited outcomes.** The same experiment did not detect effects on articulation, anticipated enjoyment of conversation, or perceived persuasion of an undecided other. It did not test sustained dialogue or directly measure reductions in polarization.
