## Overview

Network games model strategic decisions whose payoffs depend on both an agent's own action and the actions of connected neighbors. The network makes interaction structure part of the behavioral environment: the same incentives can produce different outcomes when agents occupy different topological positions.

## Key Ideas

- Strategic complements arise when a neighbor's action increases an agent's marginal return, while strategic substitutes arise when it decreases that return.
- Peer effects are the dependence of individual outcomes on group behavior. In linear-quadratic models, neighbor interactions often appear as products such as `a_{ij}x_ix_j`.
- Global congestion or competition can coexist with local peer effects. A limited resource may make agents' actions strategic substitutes even when connected neighbors reinforce one another.
- Equilibrium existence and uniqueness depend on interaction strength and network structure, commonly expressed through a spectral-radius condition.
- In homogeneous linear-quadratic games, equilibrium actions can be proportional to Bonacich centrality, so network position summarizes how direct and indirect paths transmit influence.
- External interventions can change equilibria by targeting agents, links, or incentives. Heterogeneous incentives make outcomes more flexible but also make network changes harder to interpret.
