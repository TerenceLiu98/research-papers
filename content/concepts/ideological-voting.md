## Overview

Ideological voting is vote choice guided by the policy or ideological proximity between a voter and a political alternative. In a basic spatial model, voters compare their own position with party or candidate positions and prefer the nearest option. Empirical analyses treat the strength of the relationship between ideological distance and vote probability as the degree of ideological voting.

## Key Ideas

- Proximity voting operationalizes ideological fit as distance in a policy space. A common measure is the absolute difference between voter and party placements on a left-right scale; larger distances should reduce the probability of choosing that party.
- Ideology is not the only input to vote choice. Candidate valence, competence, incumbency, name recognition, party leadership, coalition expectations, and strategic considerations can weaken or redirect proximity-based choices.
- Institutional context can change the weight of ideology. [[Electoral systems and ideological voting]] finds that voter-party distance is a stronger predictor under closed- and flexible-list proportional representation than under more candidate-centred rules.
- A single left-right scale is a useful cross-national summary but cannot represent every issue conflict. Analyses should test alternative issue dimensions where possible and distinguish the raw number of issues from [[Ideological Dimensionality|effective ideological dimensionality]].
- Ideological voting is conceptually distinct from ideological congruence. Distance can strongly predict choices even when the available parties leave many voters poorly represented, while aggregate congruence also depends on the alternatives the party system supplies.
- Stronger ideological voting is not automatically normatively superior. It can improve policy-based representation while coexisting with weaker attention to candidate quality or local representation.
