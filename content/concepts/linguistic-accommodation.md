## Overview

Linguistic accommodation describes speakers adjusting their speech toward their interlocutors. In spatial models of lexical change, one representation is positive frequency-dependent selection: locally common variants become more attractive, allowing regional conventions to persist despite movement of speakers between regions. This mathematical representation captures convergence, not every possible form of linguistic adjustment or identity-driven divergence.

## Key Ideas

- **Frequency-dependent fitness.** The linked paper uses $q_{ik}=s_{ik}+\beta x_{ik}$, with local frequency $x_{ik}$, frequency-independent bias $s_{ik}$, and accommodation factor $\beta$. Replicator dynamics increase variants whose fitness exceeds the local mean.
- **Local conformity can preserve geographic diversity.** Accommodation favors local majorities. Different regions can therefore maintain different dominant variants even though each region tends toward internal agreement.
- **Migration competes with local reinforcement.** In the linked model's unbiased, isolated, planar two-variant interface approximation with balanced incoming variants, a stationary boundary requires $\beta>2\lambda$. This is a model-specific condition rather than a universal empirical law.
- **Growth does not identify intrinsic preference.** A nationally common variant can spread through migration and local reinforcement even with negative fitted bias. Bias summarizes residual frequency-independent forces and need not isolate prestige, identity, or any single causal influence.
- **Conditional frequencies change interpretation.** If rare variants are excluded and accommodation responds to full-population frequencies, the effective coefficient for retained variants is $\beta_{\mathrm{eff}}=\beta M$, where $M$ is their combined population share. Changes in fitted accommodation can therefore reflect the observation definition as well as behavior.
