## Overview

Roll-call vote selection is the process determining which legislative decisions receive publicly accessible individual voting records. When legislators choose the recording method strategically, recorded votes can differ systematically from unrecorded votes. Estimates describing the recorded sample may then fail to characterize the broader legislative agenda.

## Key Ideas

- **Specify the target population.** All recorded votes, all final-passage votes, and all legislative decisions are different populations. Complete coverage of the first does not imply representative coverage of the others.
- **Separate prevalence from count.** Prevalence is the number of recorded votes divided by the total number of votes in the relevant population. Many roll calls can still represent a small, selectively observed share of business. Low procedural request thresholds alone do not establish high prevalence.
- **Selection can occur without changed behavior.** In Ainsley and colleagues' position-taking model, legislators vote sincerely under either procedure but request recording when their party's relative unity advantage warrants its cost. The observation process alone then distorts the sample.
- **The missing contrasts matter.** Votes dividing moderate legislators or distinguishing preference dimensions may be less attractive to record. Broad topic coverage does not ensure the voting patterns needed to identify those distinctions.
- **Cohesion error has no universal sign.** In the model, recorded votes tend to flatter the proposing party relative to its unrecorded votes and disadvantage its opponent. Across a legislative agenda, recorded cohesion can either exceed or fall below unrecorded cohesion.
- **More data need not repair selection.** The paper's simulations show persistent measurement problems when recorded-vote counts increase at roughly fixed prevalence. Higher prevalence improves recovery, but does not provide a general guarantee of representativeness.
- **Identify the level of missingness.** An entire unrecorded motion differs from one legislator's absence on a recorded motion. A model for individual nonresponse is not automatically a correction for whole-vote selection.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/roll-call-vote-selection-implications-for-the-study-of-legislative-politics|Roll-Call Vote Selection: Implications for the Study of Legislative Politics]]: combines a 145-chamber rule catalogue, prevalence evidence from 15 chambers, and simulations demonstrating selection effects on ideal points, cohesion, and dimensionality.
- Hug (2010), "Selection Effects in Roll Call Votes": identified by Ainsley and colleagues as direct evidence comparing individual behavior on votes expected to be recorded or unrecorded.
- Carrubba, Gabel, and Hug (2008), "Legislative Voting Behavior, Seen and Unseen": cited by Ainsley and colleagues for selection effects involving party discipline and changes in voting behavior.
