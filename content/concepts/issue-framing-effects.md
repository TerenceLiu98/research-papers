## Overview

Issue framing changes how people evaluate a policy by emphasizing particular attributes of that policy. In an expectancy-value account, an attitude combines evaluations of attributes with their relative salience. Emphasizing efficiency rather than public welfare can therefore shift a policy preference even without changing how positively someone evaluates either attribute.

## Key Ideas

- **Availability, accessibility, and applicability:** A consideration must be available in memory, accessible when evaluating the issue, and judged relevant. Frames can influence these processes.
- **Predispositions require a perceived connection:** A prior value constrains a response only when the audience can connect that value to the message. Context and competing frames can supply that connection.
- **Information environments matter:** Pan, Shao, and Xu argue that authoritarian mainstream consensus can suppress alternative frames that would activate resistance. Their single-frame experiment approximates this condition but does not manipulate the wider media environment.
- **Knowledge and resistance are distinct:** Knowledge can make a frame easier to understand while correlating with stronger predispositions. Their moderating roles should be examined separately.
- **Separate framing from other message effects:** Learning new facts, responding to source cues, and complying with perceived expectations can also change survey answers. A directional treatment effect alone does not identify the psychological mechanism.
- **Keep outcomes and time horizons explicit:** Immediate issue preferences, broad regime attitudes, political behavior, and durable opinion change require separate evidence. Testing opposing frames across people also differs from testing reversals within people.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/how-government-controlled-media-shifts-policy-attitudes-through-framing|How government-controlled media shifts policy attitudes through framing]]: finds that opposing state-media frames move SOE and foreign-policy preferences in a Chinese survey experiment, with some effects persisting through 48 hours.
- Chong and Druckman (2007), "Framing theory": theoretical synthesis cited by that study.
- Druckman and Nelson (2003), "Framing and deliberation: How citizens' conversations limit elite influence": cited treatment of framing, knowledge, and predisposition.
