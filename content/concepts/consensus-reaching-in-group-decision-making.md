## Overview

Consensus reaching in group decision making is the iterative process of helping multiple decision makers move from divergent preferences toward an acceptable group solution. A moderator or other external entity typically aggregates preferences, measures deviations from the group preference, and provides feedback or optimization-based adjustment guidance. Because exact agreement may be costly, many models target soft consensus within a specified tolerance.

## Key Ideas

- A basic consensus-reaching process cycles through preference elicitation, aggregation, deviation measurement, and preference adjustment until the consensus criterion is met.
- Feedback iteration mechanisms identify inconsistent decision makers and suggest adjustment directions, but may require repeated rounds and may not determine adjustment magnitudes.
- Optimization-oriented mechanisms formulate consensus as an objective problem. Minimum-cost consensus models minimize the moderator's adjustment cost, while minimum-adjustment consensus models minimize changes to decision makers' preferences.
- Social ties, trust, opinion evolution, and network position can make preference adjustments interdependent rather than independent.
- Game-based mechanisms treat adjustment as a utility problem and can use incentives or compensation to address low willingness and conflicts between individual and group concerns.
- In [[Network Game in Group Decision Making: Managing Consensus with Incentive and Interaction Interventions]], peer effects and incentives determine equilibrium adjustments, and the moderator manages consensus by changing interaction links or incentive rates.
