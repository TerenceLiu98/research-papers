## Overview

Electoral responsiveness is the extent to which changes in voter preferences or electorate composition produce changes in political representation and policy. The connection can operate by replacing officeholders or by changing the behavior of politicians already in office. Distinguishing these channels connects empirical studies of elections to competing models of political representation.

## Key Ideas

- **Extensive margin:** Voters can change policy by electing a candidate from another party. They can also select a different candidate within the same party, so conditioning only on party does not remove every selection channel.
- **Intensive margin:** A continuing officeholder may change policy positions or legislative behavior when the electorate changes. This is the direct responsiveness emphasized by Downsian competition models.
- **Partial convergence:** Detecting an intensive response does not imply that opposing candidates offer identical policies. Party differences and movement toward constituency preferences can coexist.
- **Empirical separation:** Party controls or samples with no party switching isolate within-party variation, while officeholder fixed effects more directly remove between-candidate selection. The latter identify effects among politicians observed both before and after the electoral change.
- **Measurement matters:** District composition, electoral strength, candidate vote share, and the median voter's preferences are related but distinct treatments. A design's interpretation depends on which one is changed and how it is measured.
- **Boundary changes as variation:** Redistricting can change constituency composition without residents moving, but strategic mapmaking can correlate boundary changes with expected political outcomes. Fixed effects and subgroup checks narrow this concern without making redistricting random.
- **Behavioral outcomes:** Roll-call scores provide a tractable measure of legislative positioning, but a general claim about responsiveness should consider issue-specific behavior and possible selection in recorded votes.

## Important Papers

- [[papers/how-do-voters-matter-evidence-from-us-congressional-redistricting|How Do Voters Matter? Evidence from US Congressional Redistricting]]: separates party switching, within-party candidate selection, and changes in continuing representatives' DW-NOMINATE scores after redistricting-induced shifts in predicted Democratic share.
- Lee, Moretti, and Butler (2004), "Do Voters Affect or Elect Policies? Evidence from the U.S. House": uses close elections and incumbency advantage to estimate electoral-strength effects, finding an extensive margin but no additional effect on incumbent voting behavior.
- Levitt (1996), "How Do Senators Vote? Disentangling the Role of Voter Preferences, Party Affiliation, and Senator Ideology": separates constituency, party, and individual components of senators' voting.
- Gerber and Lewis (2004), "Beyond the Median: Voter Preferences, District Heterogeneity, and Political Representation": relates district preference distributions to legislators' ideological positions.
