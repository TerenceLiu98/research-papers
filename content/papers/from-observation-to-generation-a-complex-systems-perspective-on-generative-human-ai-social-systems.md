## TL;DR

This conceptual review treats generative AI's social impact as a coupled process of behavior generation, relationship change, and collective dynamics. Its Generative Human–AI Social Systems (GHASS) framework distinguishes artificial actors used as explanatory proxies from artificial participation that constitutes the social process being studied. Agent-only societies provide experimental evidence about their own environments; transfer to human or hybrid systems requires separate validation.

## Research Question

How does artificial participation change local behavior, social relationships, and collective outcomes, and what evidence is needed to connect these levels?

## Motivation

Capability-centered accounts of language-model agents do not explain how generated behavior changes trust, exposure, coordination, or collective opinion. Relationship structure supplies the missing intermediate layer between individual outputs and system-level effects.

## Contributions

- Distinguishes three overlapping paradigms: Observation establishes empirical regularities; Simulation tests mechanisms with artificial proxies; Generation studies artificial actors participating in or mediating social processes.
- Organizes GHASS through micro-level behavior, meso-level relationships, and macro-level collective dynamics, linked by heterogeneous responses and adaptive feedback.
- Maps four relational pathways and four collective processes, then develops an agenda for cross-level validation, longitudinal stability, relational transparency, human grounding, and governance.

## Method

The structured conceptual review searches Web of Science, Scopus, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and Google Scholar, with citation chaining and manual screening. The stated final search update is August 2026. Representative historical anchors address actors, relationships, and collective outcomes together; broader chapter-level evidence may address only part of this chain. Technical agent work without a social process supplies background rather than direct GHASS evidence (Section 2).

The conceptual state is $S_t=(B_t,R_t,C_t)$: behavior, relationships, and collective dynamics. Their updates depend on one another and on platform, task, institutional, and technical conditions $E_t$. These are organizing dependencies, not fitted equations or prescribed functional forms (Section 3).

| Relational pathway | Mechanism and evidentiary role |
| --- | --- |
| AI-mediated human–human | AI changes communication, attribution of effort, sincerity, support, and common ground. |
| Human–agent | AI becomes a partner in reliance, companionship, assistance, or collaboration. |
| Agent–agent | Memory and repeated interaction produce ties and coordination within generated environments. |
| Platform-mediated | Ranking, recommendation, labeling, and moderation allocate visibility and interaction opportunities. |

Relationships include explicit edges and implicit states such as trust, influence, and perceived support. An artificial actor is defined functionally by socially consequential outputs, without implying consciousness or human-equivalent agency. GHASS specifically concerns generative AI participating in, mediating, or reshaping human social processes; earlier bots are precursors, while agent-only environments are testbeds (Sections 1, 4–5).

## Experiments

No new experiments, benchmark scores, or datasets are reported. The article synthesizes prior evidence rather than estimating a pooled effect.

- **Diffusion:** bot studies show how timing, coordination, and position affect amplification; generative agents add adaptive content production.
- **Opinion:** human communication studies support local effects on expression and deliberation, while population-level trajectories require evidence tracing repeated network interaction.
- **Norms:** agent populations exhibit conventions and cooperation, but interaction-driven emergence must be distinguished from prompt and model priors.
- **Collective intelligence:** assistance can improve individual contributions while reducing diversity or increasing dependence; group-level effects depend on participation and knowledge integration.

The proposed validation strategy combines platform observation, human experiments, and controlled agent simulations, measuring behavior, relational change, and collective outcomes in the same longitudinal design (Sections 6–7).

## Limitations

The review selects representative literature and is not an exhaustive bibliography or quantitative meta-analysis. Human experiments, platform studies, and agent simulations often support different claim levels. Realistic topology does not establish human-equivalent relational meaning; fluent behavior does not establish social validity. Nonlinear effects are hypotheses to test through state dependence and non-proportional trajectories, not properties established for every GHASS. Long-term memory, role stability, overreliance, and responsibility remain open problems.
