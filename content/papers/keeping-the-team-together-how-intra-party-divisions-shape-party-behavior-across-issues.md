## TL;DR

Across up to 260 parties in 28 European countries during 1999–2024, internal divisions are consistently associated with lower issue emphasis and greater positional ambiguity. Position change has weaker, issue-dependent support, especially when divisions are measured through candidate preference dispersion rather than expert judgments of public disagreement.

## Research Question

How do issue-specific internal divisions relate to parties’ issue salience, position changes, and position blurring?

## Motivation

Treating parties as unitary vote seekers overlooks leaders’ incentives to preserve internal unity. Avoidance, policy adjustment, and ambiguity offer different ways of managing disagreement, potentially even at an electoral cost.

## Contributions

- Integrates salience, spatial, and blurring approaches around unity seeking.
- Compares eight issues rather than restricting attention to European integration.
- Distinguishes latent preference heterogeneity from publicly perceptible disagreement using candidate and expert surveys.

## Method

CHES waves from 1999 to 2024 provide party assessments across European integration, immigration, multiculturalism, environment, redistribution, civil liberties, social lifestyle, and the economy. Coverage varies substantially by issue and outcome. Expert-rated divisions cover four issues; CCS candidate dispersion covers six. Their overlap is limited and correlations are approximately 0.15–0.20, consistent with distinct constructs.

CHES divisions use a 0–10 dissent scale. CCS divisions use within-party standard deviations of candidate issue responses, rescaled to 0–10, retaining at least ten candidates per score. CCS covers 38 elections in 18 countries; elections are matched to the next available CHES wave.

Outcomes are expert-rated issue salience, absolute position change between consecutive CHES waves, and the standard deviation of experts’ position placements. Blurring requires at least five experts and excludes 2024 because expert-level data were unavailable. It does not directly measure intentional strategy.

Issue-specific regressions include election/survey-wave fixed effects and controls for government status, vote share, and vote-share change. Pooled party-year-issue models additionally include issue fixed effects and party-clustered standard errors. Division measures are standardized in the pooled models.

## Experiments

These are observational analyses, not randomized experiments. Table 1 reports the following associations per one-standard-deviation increase in divisions; parentheses contain clustered standard errors.

| Outcome | CHES divisions | CCS divisions | Observations, CHES / CCS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Issue salience | −0.656 (0.058), p < 0.001 | −0.624 (0.078), p < 0.001 | 1,814 / 640 |
| Absolute position change | 0.151 (0.023), p < 0.001 | 0.042 (0.023), p < 0.1 | 1,814 / 1,045 |
| Position blurring | 0.218 (0.022), p < 0.001 | 0.087 (0.023), p < 0.001 | 1,197 / 1,022 |

Issue-level models support avoidance in nine of ten specifications, blurring in seven, and position change in five. Civil-liberties avoidance using CCS is not significant. Candidate-based position change is particularly fragile: support disappears with lagged outcomes and several additional controls.

Reported checks include lagged divisions, alternative outcome measures using manifestos and direct expert blurring ratings, voter-position distance, supporter heterogeneity, positional extremity, leave-one-country/issue/year-out analyses, and alternative fixed effects. Additional analyses suggest that avoidance weakens when an issue becomes more salient across the party system, and niche parties avoid divisive issues more strongly than mainstream parties.

## Limitations

Observational estimates do not establish causal effects. Shared expert measurement can generate endogeneity, and disagreement among experts may reflect difficulty locating divided parties rather than deliberate ambiguity. Candidate surveys provide a different measurement source but have narrower coverage and imperfect temporal alignment. The data cannot distinguish activist from elite divisions, intentional responses from unintended consequences, or identify how parties combine strategies. The economy is a broad dimension rather than a single issue.
