## TL;DR

The paper defines [[AI-Mediated Communication]] (AI-MC) as interpersonal communication in which a computational agent acts for a communicator by modifying, augmenting, or generating messages to achieve communication or interpersonal goals. It proposes dimensions for characterizing such systems and a research agenda spanning design, language, interpersonal dynamics, relationships, policy, and ethics.

## Research Question

How should communication research conceptualize AI that intervenes in messages exchanged between people, and what empirical, theoretical, policy, and ethical questions follow when a computational agent shares in message production?

## Motivation

Classic computer-mediated communication research treats technology primarily as the channel through which people exchange messages, while agency over message production remains with the communicator. Systems such as smart replies, automated completion, image and audio manipulation, machine translation, and generated profiles challenge that assumption because the technology can alter or produce communicative content. The authors argue that this shift requires computer-mediated communication theories to reconsider agency, mediation, interpersonal inference, and responsibility.

## Contributions

- Defines [[AI-Mediated Communication]] around computational intervention in human-to-human communication rather than interaction with an AI as the communication partner.
- Characterizes AI-MC by the magnitude of intervention, media type, optimization goal, autonomy, and role orientation, while also identifying synchronicity as an important system property.
- Establishes boundaries that exclude standalone human-agent interaction and broad ranking or recommendation algorithms that only indirectly support communication.
- Develops a multilevel research agenda covering system design, language, interpersonal dynamics, self-presentation, trust, relationships, bias, transparency, misrepresentation, and manipulation.

## Method

This is a conceptual and agenda-setting article rather than an empirical study. The authors synthesize work from computer-mediated communication, interpersonal communication, human-computer interaction, artificial intelligence, law, and ethics. They use contemporary and anticipated systems, including smart replies, automated writing assistance, machine translation, synthetic media, and receiver-oriented analysis tools, to derive a definition, system dimensions, theoretical questions, and policy concerns.

## Experiments

The paper reports no original experiment or dataset. Its empirical claims are drawn from prior studies. Examples include findings that smart-reply suggestions can trend overly positive, that perceived AI authorship can reduce trustworthiness judgments of online profiles, and that unsuccessful AI-mediated task conversations can reduce the responsibility attributed to a communication partner. These findings motivate open questions rather than test a unified model in this article.

## Limitations

The framework is an early conceptualization of a rapidly changing technical domain. Its proposed dimensions are explicitly expected to evolve, and many of its predicted effects remain open empirical questions. The definition deliberately excludes human-agent communication and broad algorithmic mediation such as feed ranking, which gives the framework analytic focus but leaves adjacent forms of AI-shaped communication outside its scope.
