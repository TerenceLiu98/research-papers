## TL;DR

Refusing alliances with far-right parties can coexist with mainstream adoption and production of exclusionary discourse and policy. Through France, Germany, and the UK, the authors argue that evaluating a cordon sanitaire requires attention to ideological normalisation as well as electoral and parliamentary cooperation.

## Research Question

Can mainstream parties claim to uphold a cordon sanitaire while advancing policies and discourses congruent with the far right? What does an electoral focus conceal about mainstream actors’ role in normalisation?

## Motivation

Debates about containment often measure success through far-right vote shares, coalition exclusion, or moderation in office. These measures can miss the influence of exclusionary ideas on policy and on targeted communities. Treating the mainstream as an automatic democratic bulwark also obscures its independent agency in producing those ideas.

## Contributions

- Distinguishes formal non-collaboration from discursive and policy separation.
- Compares three different institutional settings to show why party exclusion does not establish ideological exclusion.
- Challenges the assumption that mainstream actors merely react to far-right challengers: they can initiate and legitimise exclusionary politics themselves.

## Method

The article combines conceptual critique and a synthesis of existing scholarship with illustrative comparative case narratives. Each case contrasts electoral or parliamentary boundaries with discourse and policy. France represents a longstanding far-right challenger under two-round elections; Germany a strongly institutionalised anti-pacting norm; and the UK a narrative of exceptional immunity despite exclusionary politics. Evidence includes political statements, historical developments, secondary research, and Brexit campaign materials. This is an interpretive comparison, not a controlled estimate of the causal effect of containment.

## Experiments

There are no experiments. The empirical illustrations support the following comparisons:

| Case | Formal boundary | Discursive or policy overlap described by the authors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| France | Republican Front coordination against FN/RN, with weakening and partial revival in 2024 | Mainstream security and immigration agendas and media normalisation extend beyond changes in RN electoral fortunes. |
| Germany | Brandmauer against coalitions and legislative cooperation, challenged in Thuringia and the January 2025 migration vote | CDU and SPD migration rhetoric and policy illustrate accommodation despite commitments to exclusion. |
| UK | Exceptionalism and separation of official Brexit campaigns from UKIP | Shared narratives of national greatness and exclusion, similarities between campaign visuals, and restrictive immigration agendas across major parties undermine claims of ideological separation. |

The authors retain support for excluding far-right parties from government and media platforms. Their conclusion is that this commitment needs to encompass discourse and policy, rather than treating non-collaboration as sufficient evidence of resistance.

## Limitations

The cases illustrate a conceptual argument; they do not provide a representative comparative sample, a reproducible coded discourse corpus, or causal effect sizes. Institutional differences also prevent treating the three boundaries as equivalent interventions. Broad claims about accommodation’s electoral and social consequences draw on cited literature. The article’s interpretation of campaign imagery and mainstream rhetoric should be distinguished from direct measurement of audience responses.
