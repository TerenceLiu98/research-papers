## TL;DR

SPIRAL fine-tunes a shared language-model policy through competitive, multi-turn game self-play. Multi-game training raises the reported eight-benchmark average for Qwen3-4B-Base from 34.0 to 44.5, a 10.5 percentage-point gain, without training on target-domain problem–answer pairs. Separate game-and-role reward baselines stabilize learning.

## Research Question

Can competitive language-game self-play produce reasoning improvements that transfer to mathematics and scientific reasoning, beyond learning the training games?

## Motivation

Outcome-supervised reasoning training often requires curated questions and verifiers. Game rules supply automatic feedback, while an evolving opponent can keep the training challenge responsive to the learner's capabilities. This removes curated training questions but retains dependence on designed environments and pretrained models.

## Contributions

- An online, full-parameter, multi-turn self-play framework with a distributed actor–learner implementation.
- Role-conditioned advantage estimation to account for positional asymmetries and reduce training instability.
- Evidence of transfer from three language games to academic reasoning benchmarks and unseen game settings, with comparisons against expert-trajectory imitation and fixed opponents.

## Method

A single policy plays both roles in TicTacToe, Kuhn Poker, and Simple Negotiation, conditioned on game observations and role prompts. Each turn generates reasoning and an action; terminal game outcomes provide opposing returns. Actors use TextArena and vLLM, with learning implemented on Oat.

For each game G and role p, an exponential moving baseline is updated as b(G,p) ← α b(G,p) + (1−α) R(p). The advantage is R(p) − b(G,p). REINFORCE updates weight the log probability of full responses by this advantage, without response-length normalization. The reported EMA decay is 0.95. This is distinct from training separate policies in [[concepts/asymmetric-competitive-co-training|Asymmetric Competitive Co-Training]].

Training uses 400 steps, batches of 128, a learning rate of 10⁻⁶, and eight H100 GPUs. Appendix D reports AdamW, an 8,192-token response cap, and approximately 16–28 hours per run depending on model. The implementation is linked in the source as https://github.com/spiral-rl/spiral.

## Experiments

The eight benchmarks are MATH500, AIME24, AIME25, OlympiadBench, AMC23, Minerva Math, GPQA-Diamond, and MMLU-Pro. AIME and AMC use AVG@32; other math benchmarks use PASS@1. Table 1 marks some Qwen base-model general-reasoning scores as few-shot, despite the broader zero-shot evaluation description.

Reported averages from Table 1:

| Model | Initial | SFT-Multi | SPIRAL-Multi |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Qwen3-4B-Base | 34.0 | 39.7 | 44.5 |
| Qwen3-8B-Base | 39.5 | 46.1 | 49.6 |
| Octothinker-8B-Base | 25.8 | 27.0 | 33.8 |
| Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct | 23.9 | 25.0 | 25.9 |
| DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B | 60.4 | 58.3 | 61.8 |

SFT uses 25,000 winning trajectories generated by Qwen3-32B. Table 9 reports little additional average improvement with 52,000 SFT trajectories. Table 10 reports three-seed Qwen3-4B averages of 44.5 ± 0.5 for SPIRAL and 39.6 ± 0.4 for SFT; the displayed uncertainty should not be assigned a specific interpretation absent a clear definition.

Against Gemini-2.0-Flash, Table 5 reports a 59.5% average game win rate across six games, versus 52.9% for the strongest single-game specialist. Multi-game training is strongest on average, not on every benchmark or game. Removing role-conditioned baselines causes short or empty reasoning traces and reduced reasoning scores in the ablation.

GPT-4.1-assisted analysis of 290 game trajectories and 46,792 mathematical solutions identifies case analysis, expected-value calculation, and pattern recognition in both domains. These observations support a transfer hypothesis but do not isolate a causal mechanism.

## Limitations

The environments are engineered and relatively simple; transfer to complex open-ended tasks remains unestablished. Compute costs are substantial, and extended training shows diminishing gains. Game reward exploitation remains possible. Academic benchmark improvements do not establish general real-world competence.

The supplied manuscript contains internal inconsistencies: collapse timing varies across sections; Appendix B calls rewards dense while the method specifies terminal rewards; and some appendix prose disagrees with its tables. This summary uses Table 1 for headline averages rather than combining conflicting numbers.
