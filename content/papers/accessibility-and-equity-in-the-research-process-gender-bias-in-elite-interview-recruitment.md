## TL;DR

A preregistered audit embedded in genuine interview recruitment finds that a female-presenting email alias secured interviews with 16.3% of contacted elites, compared with 8.0% for a male-presenting alias. The reported 8.2-percentage-point difference meets the preregistered 10% significance threshold (p = 0.099), but not 5%. Conducting the promised interviews reduces the wasted time associated with fictitious audit requests.

## Research Question

Does the gender signaled by a researcher's name affect access to elite interviews, and does that effect differ by elite gender?

## Motivation

Researcher identity may shape whose voices enter qualitative evidence before an interview begins. Recruitment differences can affect both researchers' access and the composition of interview samples. Conventional audit experiments also raise ethical concerns when participants devote time to requests that will never be fulfilled.

## Contributions

- Randomizes outreach aliases within an ongoing substantive research project to study recruitment access.
- Documents a female-alias advantage contrary to the preregistered expectation of a male advantage.
- Demonstrates an embedded audit design that limits deception to identity while fulfilling interview requests.

## Method

The team invited tax professionals and OECD bureaucrats to genuine 15–30-minute semi-structured interviews about engagement on international economic issues. Firm professionals were identified through public comments; bureaucrats through OECD websites and publications. Of 229 elites emailed, 173 remained after preregistered exclusions for undelivered emails.

Identical invitations came from institutionally affiliated research-assistant aliases, Mary Williams or Jake Miller. Assignment was blocked by elite gender. The authors selected names using prior pretests to match other perceived characteristics. This design aims at [[concepts/information-equivalence|Information Equivalence]]; randomization directly identifies the contrast between these aliases, while interpretation as gender alone relies on their matching.

The primary outcome was an interview scheduled and subsequently attended. Difference-in-means tests used a preregistered significance threshold of p < 0.1. Additional models examined elite gender and interactions between gender matching and elite gender.

## Experiments

Table 1 reports the following female-alias-minus-male-alias contrasts:

| Sample | Mary Williams | Jake Miller | Reported difference | p-value | 90% CI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All elites | 16.3% (86) | 8.0% (87) | 8.2 pp | 0.099 | [0, 16] pp |
| Male elites | 17.3% (52) | 13.0% (46) | 4.3 pp | 0.56 | [−8, 16] pp |
| Female elites | 14.7% (34) | 2.4% (41) | 12.3 pp | 0.071 | [1, 23] pp |

Parentheses give sample sizes. Differences and intervals are reproduced at the source's precision; rounded rates need not subtract to the reported difference.

The result contradicts H1's predicted male-alias advantage. Separate OECD and firm estimates have the same direction but are not significant (p = 0.16 and 0.41). Male elites were more likely to participate overall, contrary to H2, but this comparison was not significant (p = 0.156).

For H3, the scheduling model reports a matched-gender coefficient of 0.123 (SE 0.066) and a matched-gender × male-elite interaction of −0.165 (SE 0.099), both significant at 10%. The matching coefficient applies to female elites; the implied effect for male elites is −0.042. Thus the model does not establish a general same-gender advantage. Corresponding matching and interaction terms for email replies were not significant.

## Limitations

The sample is small, especially for subgroup comparisons, and the overall evidence depends on the preregistered 10% threshold. Two aliases and a specific population of tax professionals and OECD bureaucrats limit generalization to other names, identities, institutions, and fields.

Support for young women and perceptions of women as less threatening are proposed explanations, not tested mechanisms. Recipient gender was not randomized. Differential recruitment may alter sample composition, but the experiment does not measure bias in the substantive conclusions of the interviews. Recommendations to diversify research teams, oversample, or send follow-ups were not experimentally evaluated.

The embedded design fulfills requests and limits deception, but still uses false identities and constrains sample size to the substantive project's needs. The supplied Markdown references supplementary robustness analyses without providing those materials.
