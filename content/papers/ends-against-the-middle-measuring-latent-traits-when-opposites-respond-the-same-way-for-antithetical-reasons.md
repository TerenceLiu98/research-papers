## TL;DR

Duck-Mayr and Montgomery introduce the generalized graded unfolding model (GGUM) to political measurement and develop Metropolis-coupled MCMC estimation with posterior orientation alignment. The model allows opposition to a moderate item from both ideological extremes. Survey, Supreme Court, and congressional applications show better response fit and placements more consistent with the authors' substantive evidence than monotonic models. Under simulated monotonic responses, GGUM and conventional IRT recover almost identical positions. The method remains one-dimensional and does not identify the strategic reasons for dissent.

## Research Question

How can categorical responses reveal latent political positions when actors at opposite extremes give the same answer for opposing reasons, and how can the resulting multimodal posterior be estimated reliably?

## Motivation

A moderate immigration statement can attract disagreement from respondents who want either stricter or more permissive policy. Similarly, liberal and conservative justices may dissent from the same ruling, and legislators on both sides may oppose a compromise. Monotonic response models can treat such items as weakly informative or place ideological extremists near the middle. [[concepts/generalized-graded-unfolding-model|Generalized Graded Unfolding Model]] response curves make proximity to an item's position informative even when agreement is not monotonic in ideology.

## Contributions

- Adapts an existing psychometric unfolding model, originally developed by Roberts, Donoghue, and Laughlin (2000), to political survey and voting data.
- Develops Metropolis-coupled MCMC (MC3) estimation to explore separated posterior modes, with identification imposed during post-processing.
- Implements the estimator in the R package `bggum` and reports simulation comparisons with marginal maximum likelihood and earlier MCMC estimation.
- Demonstrates how two-sided disagreement can inform one-dimensional measurement in three political applications.

## Method

### Folding Hypothetical Responses

For respondent position $\theta_i$ and item location $\delta_j$, the model distinguishes hypothetical responses from below and above the item. Observed agreement pools agreement from both sides; observed disagreement similarly pools two different latent responses. Item discrimination $\alpha_j$ controls response sensitivity, while threshold parameters $\tau_{jk}$ govern hypothetical category transitions. Symmetric thresholds produce response functions centered on the item location (Section 3, Equations 1-2).

This construction assumes symmetric, single-peaked proximity preferences. An extreme item location can place the turning point outside the respondents' observed range, yielding effectively monotonic responses. Relative to the standard IRT models discussed in the paper, the specification adds one parameter per item. Unfolding does not imply nonmonotonicity in every spatial voting problem: sincere choice between two alternatives under concave utility can still yield monotonic vote probabilities.

### Estimation and Identification

The model uses standard normal priors on respondent positions and bounded four-parameter Beta priors on item parameters. MC3 runs chains at different inverse temperatures, with Metropolis-Hastings updates and exchanges between adjacent chains. Heated chains help explore separated modes; only draws from the cold chain targeting the posterior are retained for inference.

The posterior has both substantive multimodality and reflection symmetry. The standard normal prior fixes the latent scale, while post-processing aligns reflected draws using the sign of a relatively extreme respondent or item location. This is a one-dimensional instance of [[concepts/rotational-invariance-in-ideal-point-models|Rotational Invariance in Ideal-Point Models]]. Orientation alignment does not eliminate substantively distinct modes. The authors report that conventional sign restrictions during sampling do not adequately address GGUM's posterior geometry.

## Experiments

### Simulations

For 100 respondents and 400 binary items generated by the Clinton-Jackman-Rivers (CJR) monotonic model, latent estimates correlate at 0.999. Table 1 reports log likelihoods of -18,989 for CJR and -19,021 for GGUM, with mean posterior position standard deviation 0.11 for both. This demonstrates near-equivalence in that monotonic setting, with slightly better fit for the generating model.

The main text reports that supplementary comparisons find lower parameter RMSE than marginal maximum likelihood and better movement between modes than the earlier GGUM MCMC routine. A separate two-dimensional simulation yields correlations above 0.99 between GGUM estimates and the first dimension of the two-dimensional model; it does not show that GGUM recovers both dimensions. The authors acknowledge that a second dimension correlated with first-dimension extremity can still resemble two-sided disagreement.

### Immigration Survey

A ten-item battery uses five ordered response categories and includes both moderate and extreme immigration statements. GGUM captures two-sided disagreement for moderate items that have nearly flat response functions under a graded response model (GRM). The two models' position estimates correlate at 0.936. Correlations with self-reported ideology are 0.627 for GGUM and 0.618 for GRM, a small external-association difference (Section 5.1).

### Supreme Court and House Votes

The Court analysis covers 203 nonunanimous cases during the natural court from Kagan's appointment to Scalia's death. The House analysis includes 438 members and 846 roll calls from the 116th Congress, requiring a minority vote of at least 1% and member participation in at least 10% of included votes. The House estimation uses two recorded chains, each supported by six temperature chains, with 10,000 burn-in and 100,000 recorded iterations.

Table 2 reports the following fit and uncertainty summaries. Log likelihood comparisons are within each dataset; larger, less negative values indicate better fit.

In *Comptroller of the Treasury of Maryland v. Wynne*, GGUM accommodates a centrist majority opposed by ideologically separated dissents. In *Arizona v. United States*, both approaches yield similar, effectively monotonic response functions. For House members, GGUM places Ocasio-Cortez, Omar, Pressley, and Tlaib at the liberal extreme rather than the moderate positions obtained under CJR, consistent with the qualitative evidence discussed by the authors. The conclusion reports that supplementary analysis classifies nearly 17% of 116th House roll calls as nonmonotonic.

## Limitations

- The implementation is unidimensional. Nonmonotonicity and [[concepts/ideological-dimensionality|Ideological Dimensionality]] are distinct issues, but can be confounded in particular data-generating processes.
- Symmetric proximity preferences and the interpretation of an item as an object of approval are substantive assumptions. The model does not determine whether dissent reflects sincerity, electoral signaling, or bargaining strategy.
- Multimodal posteriors can remain difficult to identify and summarize, especially in more complex multiparty settings. Bounded item priors can truncate posterior support; the authors recommend checking bounds and varying hyperparameters.
- MC3 increases computational cost. In the Court application, the reported runtime is 42 minutes 8 seconds versus 14 minutes 56 seconds for the Martin-Quinn implementation.
- Reported likelihood improvements and narrower posterior intervals describe fitted models; they do not independently establish true ideological positions or calibrated uncertainty under misspecification.
- The supplied Markdown omits the supplementary analyses and full footnotes and contains malformed mathematical notation. Appendix findings above are attributed to the main text. Section 5.3 says "lower" log likelihoods, but Table 2 gives higher values for GGUM; the table values are preserved here.
