## TL;DR

An inductive study of Alex Jones's promotion and monetization of false Sandy Hook counternarratives develops a model of [[Conspiracy Entrepreneurship]]. Its central mechanism, [[Perpetuated Sensegiving]], keeps audiences interpreting an event by repeatedly introducing purported evidence and reopening questions. The model distinguishes sustained ambiguity about the event from reduced ambiguity about the venture's credibility and products. The evidence supports a qualitative process account, not a causal estimate of how ambiguity affects purchases or a determination of the entrepreneur's underlying intentions.

The supplied manuscript identifies itself as an accepted, forthcoming authors' version in *Academy of Management Journal*. It supplies no publication year or stable paper identifier; the year is therefore left unspecified.

## Research Question

How does an individual guide the construction of a conspiracy theory that can be exploited for commercial gain, and how can audience engagement persist without convergence on a settled account?

## Motivation

Sensemaking research commonly examines how people construct workable interpretations of ambiguous events, while cultural entrepreneurship research explains how stories establish legitimacy and attract resources. This case asks how commercial opportunities can instead depend on repeatedly renewing ambiguity. The puzzle concerns the production and monetization of unresolved interpretation, beyond whether audiences believe or transmit a particular false claim.

## Contributions

- Develops a process model linking the creation of a conspiratorial arena, audience mobilization, perpetuated sensegiving, and monetization.
- Identifies recurring practices that renew ambiguity: introducing purported evidence, connecting events to preexisting narratives about hidden actors, and posing open-ended questions under an appearance of caution.
- Distinguishes ambiguity about an event from ambiguity about a venture. A broadcaster can destabilize interpretations of the former while presenting the latter as a credible, recognizable media operation.
- Proposes that participation and consumption can affirm an audience's identity as investigators, sustaining engagement without agreement on a final explanation.
- Differentiates perpetuated sensegiving from strategic ambiguity through its continuing renewal of questions, direction toward nonmainstream interpretations, and role in sustaining a commercial opportunity.

## Method

The authors use a single revelatory case centered on *Lafferty et al. v. Jones* (2022), which involved 13 plaintiffs. Their historical analysis covers 2012-2017, emphasizing 2012-2014 and using 2015-2017 material to supplement the account. The dates of litigation records are distinct from the period of behavior reconstructed.

| Evidence source | Reported scope | Analytic use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Court archive | 1,238 documents filed from May 2018 to November 2022 | Reconstructs the counternarrative, commercial practices, and harms through filings, testimony, and other records |
| Broadcast archive | 960 transcripts from 2012-2015; keyword search identified 244 episodes, of which 140 were retained | Traces substantive Sandy Hook discussions, call-ins, guests, and product promotion; Table 1 reports more than 60 hours |
| Secondary archive | News reporting, documentaries, trial videos, and books | Provides context and additional accounts of public reception and commercialization |

Because transcripts did not label speakers, the first author listened to recordings to distinguish Jones, callers, and guests and recorded contextual notes. Relevant passages and audio were examined together, and transcripts were coded in NVivo. Analysis moved iteratively from first-order open codes to second-order categories and theoretical dimensions, retaining attention to temporal sequence. Tables 3-6 provide illustrative evidence; Appendices B-E contain an interaction excerpt, a process illustration, the coding structure, and a chronology of counternarrative elements.

The resulting model comprises four connected processes, rather than a strictly linear sequence:

1. **Create a conspiratorial arena.** Professional broadcast conventions and invitations to contribute information make the platform appear credible and participatory.
2. **Mobilize an audience.** Validate suspicion of mainstream reporting, feature apparently authoritative guests, and encourage investigation.
3. **Perpetuate sensegiving.** Reintroduce purported evidence, invoke wider conspiratorial narratives, and pose further questions so that tentative explanations do not bring inquiry to a close.
4. **Monetize engagement.** Present purchases as support for investigation or preparation for threats, insert advertisements at moments of suspended revelation, and use engagement and sales spikes to inform programming.

## Experiments

There are no controlled experiments, benchmark comparisons, or statistical tests of the proposed mechanism. Evidence comes from qualitative analysis of archival records and recorded interactions.

The findings document exchanges in which Jones redirected callers seeking explanations or resolution toward renewed suspicion and vigilance. Broadcast sequences place product promotions immediately after purported revelations or unresolved inconsistencies. The authors interpret these patterns as linking audience sensemaking to consumption. Court and secondary records also document harassment and threats toward victims' families associated with the audience's purported investigative activity.

Commercial figures are reported contextual evidence, not independently estimated treatment effects:

- Citing Spectrum News (2022), the manuscript reports that after a September 24, 2014 article falsely claimed that nobody died at Sandy Hook, website sessions rose from roughly 543,000 to nearly one million the following day, while daily online-store revenues rose from $48,000 to over $230,000.
- Citing the court documents, it reports $165 million in Infowars store sales during 2015-2018. This is aggregate store revenue, not profit or an amount attributable exclusively to Sandy Hook content.
- Testimony and records describe monitoring visitor and sales analytics and trying to reproduce previous spikes. The authors nevertheless state that they did not directly examine the proposed reinforcing relationship between monetization and subsequent sensegiving.

## Limitations

- **Case scope:** The account derives from one prominent broadcaster in a particular US political and media environment. Transfer to other actors, countries, or venture lifecycles remains a research question.
- **Audience conditions:** The proposed mechanism is most plausible where continued inquiry supplies identity, purpose, or perceived agency, especially amid polarization and institutional distrust. These are proposed boundary conditions, not experimentally tested moderators.
- **Intentionality:** The authors study observable practices and consequences. They do not establish whether the patterns were deliberately strategic or emerged through adaptation, despite strategic language elsewhere in the manuscript.
- **Revenue feedback:** Programming testimony suggests a feedback process, but the authors explicitly leave its direct examination to future research.
- **Inference from archives:** Selected public interactions and litigation materials do not represent all audience members or directly establish their private motives. Reported traffic and revenue changes do not isolate the causal contribution of ambiguity, identity, or specific broadcasts.
- **Practical implications:** Recognizing these communication patterns may inform interventions, but the study does not test a strategy for disrupting them.
