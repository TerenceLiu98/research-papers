## TL;DR

Sandler and George synthesize sixty years of alliance economics through pure public deterrence, spatial connectivity, and joint production of defense benefits. Predictions about [[concepts/alliance-burden-sharing|Alliance Burden-Sharing]], free riding, efficiency, and membership depend on the publicness and geographic reach of defense outputs. Updated NATO rank tests show that disproportionate burdens and benefit–burden concordance are distinct empirical questions.

## Research Question

How do strategic doctrine, technology, threats, and membership alter the defense benefits allies receive, and therefore their expenditure responses, burden sharing, and institutional needs?

## Motivation

The original public-good model predicts that wealthy allies carry disproportionate burdens while smaller allies free ride. Historical variation in NATO does not consistently fit that prediction. Conventional protection, national benefits, and geographically uneven spillovers require broader models; expenditure levels and expenditure-to-GDP ratios also answer different questions.

## Contributions

- Derives defense demand from three related theoretical frameworks and explains their different policy implications.
- Connects changes in NATO doctrine and membership to the mix of public and excludable defense benefits.
- Updates descriptive spending comparisons and rank-based burden-sharing tests through 2023.
- Synthesizes spatial, operational-category, and non-NATO research, emphasizing specification choices and heterogeneous responses.

## Method

This is a selective theoretical retrospective with updated descriptive and nonparametric evidence, rather than an exhaustive systematic review.

In the pure public model, ally i chooses defense q_i to maximize utility from private consumption and total defense q_i + Q_−i, subject to income financing consumption and defense. Other allies’ spending Q_−i is substitutable for its own. A Nash contributor internalizes only its own marginal benefit, whereas efficient provision accounts for benefits to all allies, producing an underprovision problem.

Spatial models replace unweighted spillins with a weighted sum of other allies’ spending. Membership, contiguity, inverse distance, power projection, and economic ties represent different protection channels. Joint product models instead allow spending to generate both national benefits and shared deterrence. Complementarity between those outputs can produce positive expenditure responses to spillins. Conventional protection can also be partly rival through force thinning, making optimal membership depend on the cost of defending additional territory.

The derived demand equations use military expenditure (ME), with income, spillins, controls, and threats as explanatory variables. Simultaneous expenditure choices make spillins endogenous. The authors distinguish this model from regressions of ME/GDP, where positive interdependence can represent burden convergence rather than the absence of free riding.

## Experiments

The paper reports observational summaries and rank tests, not controlled experiments. Spending uses SIPRI data in constant 2022 US dollars; GDP uses World Development Indicators. Descriptive comparisons cover 1961–2023, with Russian spending comparisons beginning in 1991.

Table 1 tests the exploitation hypothesis using Spearman correlations between ME/GDP and GDP, including partial correlations controlling for GDP per capita and exposed borders. The simple correlation is 0.600 (p = 0.023) in 1965, 0.270 (p = 0.350) in 1970, and 0.546 (p = 0.035) in 1985. The pattern supports historically variable disproportionality rather than a universal income-based rule.

Table 2 compares each ally’s share of NATO spending with equally weighted benefit proxies: shares of GDP, population, and exposed borders, with two alternatives adding terrorism exposure. Wilcoxon tests reject concordance in all displayed years from 2000 through 2023. Although the prose states rejection at 1% throughout, the 2000 second-proxy result is p = 0.014, supporting rejection at 5% instead. Reported p-values of 0.000 are rounded values.

The literature synthesis distinguishes its findings from these updated tests. Spatial studies using expenditure levels commonly find negative responses to allied spillins. Reviewed operational evidence finds the most free riding in equipment, followed by operations and maintenance and personnel; immobile infrastructure permits the least substitution. Non-NATO applications show that aggregation technology, geography, and local threats alter these patterns.

## Limitations

- Benefit shares are proxies with equal weights because preferences over protection are unknown; rejection of concordance does not directly measure the share of public benefits.
- Historical spending patterns and rank tests do not identify causal effects of strategic doctrine or alliance expansion.
- The baseline models simplify decision making, costs, timing, and output substitution. Empirical conclusions depend on spatial weights, the dependent variable, and treatment of simultaneous choices.
- The review identifies enemy geography, adversaries’ operational spending, and an integrated model of within-alliance burden sharing and between-alliance arms races as unfinished research.
- The supplied text contains extraction artifacts and the Table 2 significance discrepancy noted above; no replication results are claimed here.
