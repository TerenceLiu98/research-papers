## TL;DR

Iwanami develops a three-player complete-information game in which a status-quo superpower chooses whether and how much to support a potentially revisionist ally before the ally and a non-signatory arm and bargain. [[alliance-burden-sharing|Alliance Burden-Sharing]] can preserve the status quo through either restraint or deterrence, but the two effects occupy different ranges of the ally's armament cost. A heavily contributing superpower can induce a relatively low-cost ally to remain weak and accept the status quo; with a high-cost ally, calibrated support can instead induce allied rearmament sufficient to deter the non-signatory. These are conditional model results, illustrated through postwar Japanese rearmament rather than tested causally.

## Research Question

How can a status-quo-oriented superpower use alliance formation and defense burden-sharing to restrain a potentially revisionist ally, deter an external challenger, and avoid provoking that challenger?

## Motivation

Alliance research commonly treats deterrence, allied military capabilities, and alliance commitments as given. That leaves unresolved why a capable prospective ally would accept dependence and restraint, how alliance formation changes members' armament choices, and whether capability aggregation necessarily threatens non-signatories. The paper therefore makes alliance formation and all three states' military investments endogenous.

## Contributions

- Connects alliance restraint and deterrence within one model of endogenous alliance formation, arms buildup, and bargaining.
- Shows that restraint and deterrence are mutually exclusive equilibrium effects across distinct ranges of the prospective ally's marginal armament cost.
- Explains why asymmetric burden-sharing can restrain an ally when the superpower's contribution makes military weakness preferable to unilateral revision.
- Derives a deterrence mechanism in which the superpower supplies only enough protection to induce the ally to strengthen its own forces, rather than automatically permitting free riding.
- Separates an alliance's aggregate capabilities from the ally's unilateral threat: power aggrandizement need not provoke the non-signatory when the ally remains unable to revise the status quo alone.

## Method

The paper solves a sequential, three-player complete-information game for subgame-perfect Nash equilibria by backward induction. Country Z is a status-quo-oriented superpower; A is its prospective ally and may seek revisions against B; B may also seek revisions against A.

In the military-spending phase, Z moves first. A positive contribution forms an alliance, after which A and B choose costly military capabilities. In the bargaining phase, B proposes a division to A; A may accept, reject, or attack, and B may fight after rejection. Z intervenes only if B attacks A. Contest success depends on A and Z's combined forces relative to B's, while each state pays a linear armament cost and war costs where applicable.

Without an alliance, low armament costs lead A to build enough strength to extract concessions from B; intermediate costs support the status quo; high costs leave A too weak to prevent concessions to B. Z forms an alliance only when doing so changes one of the latter two revisionary outcomes into the status quo.

### Restraining Region

When A's armament cost is relatively low, Z may shoulder a disproportionately large burden. This support reduces A's incentive to maintain forces capable of unilateral revision, leaving A dependent on the alliance and making acceptance of the status quo preferable. If A's cost is extremely low, however, Z cannot prevent revision and does not form the alliance.

### Deterrence Region

When A's armament cost is relatively high, Z contributes enough to make additional allied investment worthwhile. A then strengthens its forces until the alliance can deter B. Z's equilibrium contribution is calibrated to induce this response, rather than to replace A's contribution completely.

## Experiments

The paper reports comparative-statics and equilibrium results, not statistical experiments. Its central predictions are:

| Ally's armament cost | Outcome without alliance | Superpower response | Alliance effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Extremely low | A revises the status quo | No alliance because support cannot restrain A | None |
| Relatively low | A arms heavily and B concedes | Z bears a heavy burden, inducing A to remain weak | Restraint |
| Moderate | Status quo already prevails | No alliance, avoiding unnecessary support | None |
| Relatively high | A remains weak and concedes to B | Z provides enough support to induce A's rearmament | Deterrence |

The historical section uses the US-Japan relationship from 1945 to 1960 as an illustration. It interprets early occupation and demilitarization as restraint, followed by a shift toward deterrence as Cold War threats increased. Japan's economic weakness, Article 9, and domestic opposition raised the cost of rearmament; US assistance and pressure accompanied the gradual creation of the National Police Reserve, National Safety Force, and Self-Defense Forces. The 1960 treaty strengthened the US defense commitment while Japan increased its forces and US troop levels declined. This chronology illustrates the model's mechanisms but does not estimate or identify them.

## Limitations

- The equilibrium predictions depend on complete information, Z moving first, linear armament costs, and credible superpower commitments.
- The intervention rule is asymmetric: Z helps A only when B attacks, so alternative alliance obligations could change the results.
- The model compresses heterogeneous domestic constraints into a marginal cost of armament and does not model how those constraints arise or change.
- The postwar Japan discussion is a historical illustration, not a causal test of the model. The online appendix reportedly adds West German cases, but it was not included in the supplied Markdown.
- Restraint and deterrence occur in separate parameter regions, so the model does not predict both effects being observed simultaneously in a single equilibrium.

## Related Papers

The paper directly builds on:

- Olson and Zeckhauser (1966), "An Economic Theory of Alliances": develops the public-good and free-riding account that Iwanami qualifies by deriving superpower contributions designed to induce allied effort.
- Morrow (1991), "Alliances and Asymmetry": supplies the security-autonomy tradeoff behind the link between asymmetric contributions, dependence, and influence.
- Fang, Johnson, and Leeds (2014), "To Concede or to Resist? The Restraining Effect of Military Alliances": models alliance restraint with exogenous capabilities, which this paper endogenizes.
- Yuen (2009), "Target Concessions in the Shadow of Intervention": connects third-party intervention to concessions and alliance restraint.
- Benson, Meirowitz, and Ramsay (2014), "Inducing Deterrence through Moral Hazard in Alliance Contracts": analyzes deterrence and incentive problems in alliance commitments.

Within the library, [[bipolarity-multipolarity-and-the-future|Bipolarity, multipolarity, and the future]] also examines how major-power influence and alliance relationships may restrain conflict, although it studies system structure rather than endogenous defense contributions. This is a thematic connection, not a citation-based equivalence.
