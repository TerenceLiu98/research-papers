## TL;DR

This Perspective introduces [[concepts/cross-stage-dependence|Cross-Stage Dependence]]: different stages of scientific selection may reuse earlier signals or shared evaluative infrastructure rather than supply independent reasons for supporting research. It hypothesizes that, under uncertainty, this dependence can narrow exploration and slow redirection even while output grows. It presents a conceptual framework and testable proposals, not original empirical results.

## Research Question

Do entry, allocation, production, and retention preserve distinct routes for scientific exploration and evaluation, or repeatedly reinforce the same selections?

## Motivation

Publication volume and institutional plurality do not establish breadth of search. Funding, attention, and verification remain scarce, and abandoning expertise or infrastructure can make neglected directions expensive to recover. AI can increase production while concentrating effort on computationally tractable questions or spreading common assumptions across generation and evaluation.

## Contributions

- Organizes scientific search into four functional stages: entry into careers, allocation of resources, production of research, and retention through publication, attention, replication, and reuse.
- Distinguishes cross-stage dependence from cumulative advantage, path dependence, and simple agreement among evaluators.
- Proposes identification strategies and a conditional prediction connecting dependence to reduced adaptive exploration.
- Develops design principles: preserve alternative routes under uncertainty, permit evidence-driven convergence, retain reversible commitments, and diversify verification.

## Method

The paper synthesizes prior studies of careers, grants, teams, evaluation, and AI. A selector is any mechanism changing which research possibilities are explored, supported, transmitted, or retained; institutions can span several stages.

Section 4 illustrates dependence with an early grant that increases output and visibility, which later evaluators treat as evidence of promise. The empirical target is later selection attributable to inherited signals or shared inputs beyond independently available scientific evidence. Agreement based on strong evidence alone is not dependence. “Effective diversity” is descriptive shorthand for nonredundant routes, not a proposed universal index.

Suggested designs exploit funding thresholds, lotteries, or policy changes; experimentally reveal or conceal prior funding, affiliation, and status; or compare institutions sharing different criteria and infrastructure. Under substantial uncertainty and comparable visible diversity, greater dependence is predicted to eliminate alternatives faster and impede response to contradictory evidence. Null or beneficial effects on adaptive exploration would count against this prediction.

Section 5 separates AI's effects on capacity, direction, and dependence. Shared models may couple hypothesis generation and evaluation, but diverse tools could also open neglected directions. The net effect remains an empirical question.

## Experiments

No original experiments, datasets, or quantitative estimates are reported. Cited studies motivate possible channels: Bol et al. (2018) on early funding advantages, Huber et al. (2022) on author-status effects, and Boudreau et al. (2016) on evaluator distance and novelty. Countervailing evidence includes Jacob and Lefgren (2011), whose modest publication effect of NIH funding is consistent with substitution toward other funding sources. These studies do not establish system-wide excessive dependence.

## Limitations

Scientific quality is only partially observable, complicating separation of inherited signals from justified convergence. The framework supplies neither a universal dependence metric nor an empirical estimate of its prevalence. AI-induced dependence and impaired adaptation are hypotheses. Maintaining alternatives has costs, and dependence is not intrinsically harmful when selectors share reliable evidence. The supplied manuscript gives no explicit publication year or stable identifier; the year is left unspecified.
