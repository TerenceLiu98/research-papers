## TL;DR

The paper models economic growth as a race between automation, which transfers existing tasks from labor to capital, and the creation of new tasks in which labor has a comparative advantage. Automation lowers employment and labor's income share, while new tasks raise both. When innovation responds endogenously to factor prices, the two forces can converge to a stable balanced-growth path, although sufficiently cheap capital can instead produce full automation and labor-market frictions can bias innovation toward excessive automation.

## Research Question

When does automation make labor redundant, and when can the creation of new labor-intensive tasks sustain wages, employment, and the labor share as technology and capital accumulation evolve?

## Motivation

Predictions of technological unemployment often treat labor-replacing innovation as the only relevant margin. Historical technological change also created occupations and more complex tasks in which people retained comparative advantage. A framework that jointly models these two margins is needed to distinguish temporary displacement, permanent shifts in factor shares, and the limiting case in which labor becomes redundant.

## Contributions

- Builds a [[concepts/task-based-models-of-automation|task-based model of automation]] in which factor prices and technology jointly determine which tasks capital and labor perform.
- Separates automation from the creation of new tasks and shows that they have opposing effects on employment and labor's share of income.
- Embeds the static allocation in a growth model with capital accumulation, then endogenizes research through [[concepts/directed-technological-change|directed technological change]].
- Characterizes full-automation, no-automation, and interior balanced-growth paths, including conditions for uniqueness and stability.
- Extends the model to skill inequality, creative destruction, and welfare under labor-market frictions.

## Method

Final output is a constant-elasticity aggregate of a unit interval of tasks. Tasks below an automation frontier can be produced by capital, while all tasks can be produced by labor with productivity increasing in task complexity. Firms choose the cheaper feasible factor, so the technologically automatable set need not equal the set actually performed by capital.

The static model compares shifts in the automation frontier with shifts in the frontier of available tasks. The dynamic model adds household saving, endogenous capital, and balanced growth. In the full model, a fixed supply of scientists chooses between inventing automation technologies and creating new tasks according to their expected values. Relative factor costs therefore direct innovation and determine the gap between the two frontiers.

## Experiments

This is primarily a theoretical paper rather than an empirical evaluation. Its motivating descriptive analysis combines occupational employment growth from 1980 to 2015 with Lin's measures of new job titles. The paper reports that occupations with a 10-percentage-point larger share of new titles in 1980 experienced 0.41 percent faster annual employment growth, and attributes about 60 percent of roughly 50 million added US jobs to the additional growth of occupations containing new titles. This association motivates the new-task mechanism; it is not presented as a causal estimate.

The model's main comparative results are:

- With fixed capital, automation reduces employment and the labor share and can reduce wages; new tasks raise all three.
- With endogenous capital adjustment, automation still lowers long-run employment and the labor share but raises the long-run wage through its productivity effect.
- In a stable interior balanced-growth path, a temporary automation shock induces innovation toward new tasks and can restore employment and the labor share. A permanent improvement in automation research instead leads to a new path with lower employment and labor share.
- Both automation and new tasks can initially increase skill inequality. If new tasks eventually become fully standardized, their long-run effect can reverse; incomplete standardization allows inequality to diverge.
- Without labor-market frictions, both forms of innovation raise welfare. With a wedge between wages and labor's opportunity cost, automation can reduce welfare and decentralized research can be excessively automation-oriented.

## Limitations

- The empirical evidence on new job titles is descriptive and does not identify the model's mechanisms or estimate its structural parameters.
- Tasks are ordered by complexity and automation proceeds from the bottom of that ordering; the model does not accommodate selective automation of middle tasks.
- Technological feasibility and task creation are not modeled at the industry level, despite likely sectoral barriers and differences.
- New tasks are the model's sole labor-complementing innovation, leaving other forms of complementarity outside the framework.
- The standardization extension abstracts from explicit schooling, retraining, and other adjustment costs that may delay workers' movement into new tasks.
- Stability under creative destruction requires additional restrictions; with constant automation difficulty, an interior balanced-growth path is not stable.

## Related Papers

- Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003), "The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change": a foundational empirical task-based account of routine-task computerization and labor-market inequality.
- Acemoglu and Autor (2011), "Skills, Tasks and Technologies: Implications for Employment and Earnings": the closest antecedent to the paper's static task-allocation framework.
- Zeira (1998), "Workers, Machines, and Economic Growth": an earlier growth model based on capital-labor substitution across technologies.
- Hémous and Olsen (2016), "The Rise of the Machines: Automation, Horizontal Innovation and Income Inequality": studies endogenous automation and inequality without the same long-run countervailing new-task mechanism.
