## TL;DR

Aryal argues that the rise of Asian emerging powers, middle-power agency, and increasingly autonomous regional orders disperses international influence without necessarily dismantling the [[liberal-international-order|Liberal International Order]]. Because these states have benefited from existing economic and institutional arrangements, the article interprets their demands primarily as efforts to gain influence and reform governance. This is a qualitative interpretive argument, not a causal estimate or a demonstrated forecast of the order's survival.

## Research Question

How does the rise of the Global South reshape Asian [[international-polarity|polarity]], and do the resulting power configurations seek to reform, challenge, or replace the liberal international order?

## Motivation

Debates framed as Western decline versus the rise of the rest can equate reduced US dominance with the collapse of liberal institutions. The article treats Asia as a central setting for examining that assumption, given China's and India's ascent, the influence of middle powers, and regional institutions that constrain how outside powers engage. It also questions accounts that reduce regional politics to a US-China confrontation.

## Contributions

- Distinguishes unipolar, bipolar, and multipolar distributions of power from the broader governance framework of a [[multiplex-world-order|Multiplex World Order]].
- Connects Global South influence to changes in economic dependencies, supply chains, and institutional decision-making.
- Organizes contemporary Asian polarity around emerging powers, middle powers, and regional orders as a distinct level of analysis.
- Argues that demands for greater representation can reshape the LIO from within, without implying a coherent replacement order.

## Method

The author describes the approach as positivist-qualitative, using primary and secondary sources to interpret observable states, institutions, and power distributions. The article combines a conceptual review of polarity with a historical account of Asian Cold War relations and contemporary examples of economic and diplomatic change. It does not specify a systematic source-selection protocol, a coded dataset, or a formal causal identification strategy.

The historical discussion covers the Chinese Civil War, the Korean and Vietnam wars, and the Non-Aligned Movement. It emphasizes that the absence of direct superpower war did not mean peace in Asian societies affected by proxy conflict. The contemporary assessment examines China, India, and Indonesia as emerging powers; actors such as Japan, South Korea, Vietnam, and the Philippines as influential regional stakeholders; and ASEAN and India's regional position as constraints on external powers.

## Experiments

There are no experiments or statistical hypothesis tests. The evidence consists of historical interpretation, selected economic indicators, and institutional examples.

| Evidence discussed | Interpretation advanced in the article |
| --- | --- |
| Sino-Russian trade reported at USD 240 billion in FY2023 and India-Russia trade rising from roughly USD 10-13 billion before the war to USD 65 billion | Commercial choices by emerging powers constrain Western efforts to isolate Russia. These are cited figures, not original estimates of sanctions effects. |
| Supply-chain diversification toward India, Vietnam, and Mexico | Reduced dependence on China can coexist with continued dependence on production in the Global South. |
| BRICS arrangements and demands for greater representation in international institutions | Emerging powers combine participation in existing institutions with attempts to redistribute authority. |
| ASEAN norms and centrality, alongside India's position in South Asia and the Indian Ocean | Regional actors can influence how great powers operate within regional orders. |

The conclusion interprets continued benefits from trade, institutions, and security arrangements as incentives for reform rather than replacement. It also argues that no coherent alternative order is currently articulated. Both are the author's assessments within the article's historical setting.

## Limitations

- The selected cases illustrate the argument but do not isolate the effect of multipolarity on institutional reform, stability, or LIO durability.
- No operational threshold establishes when an emerging or middle power becomes a pole. The discussion moves between multipolar and multiplex descriptions without an empirical comparison of their explanatory performance.
- The inference from benefiting within existing institutions to intending to preserve them is not established through a systematic assessment of state preferences. Selective cooperation and institutional contestation can coexist.
- The article acknowledges that the Global South is a contested category, including disagreement over China's membership, and that Asia contains diverse regional trajectories.
- Economic figures come from cited sources with differing dates and scopes. They should be read as reported illustrations rather than a harmonized capability dataset or current statistics.
