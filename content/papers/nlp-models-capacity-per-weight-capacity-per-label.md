## TL;DR

This paper argues that capacity per label (CpL) is more informative than capacity per weight (CpW) for NLP pre-training and fine-tuning tasks with many output labels and nonzero error. Committee-machine simulations show that CpW is nearly independent of hidden-layer count but decreases as the number of outputs grows. BERT experiments then show that substantial pruning can preserve accuracy, with the maximal pruning level largely independent of training-set size. A toy archetype model explains these observations and motivates CpL as the maximum architecture dilution that preserves the undiluted model's accuracy for a fixed number of labels.

## Research Question

Does the number of trainable weights provide a useful measure of NLP task complexity, or should capacity be normalized by the number of output labels and evaluated at finite accuracy?

## Motivation

Classical CpW measures the number of random patterns a network can classify perfectly relative to its trainable weights. NLP models instead predict among many tokens or task labels, usually retain positive loss, and are often evaluated with finite accuracy. The paper is motivated by simulations in which BERT preserves accuracy after heavy pruning and tolerates similar pruning across datasets with similar token counts despite different paragraph counts. These observations challenge a direct interpretation of CpW as NLP task complexity.

## Contributions

- Estimates CpW for restricted committee machines using a modified back-propagation procedure and approaches the one-step replica-symmetry-breaking estimate.
- Shows in toy feedforward models that CpW and finite-error accuracy are largely insensitive to adding hidden layers, while accuracy decreases as the number of output units increases.
- Introduces CpL through a fixed-size noisy-archetype classification problem, defining capacity through the maximal dilution that preserves undiluted accuracy.
- Demonstrates the two motivating patterns in BERT-6 masked-language-model pre-training: substantial pruning can preserve accuracy per token (APT), and the preserving dilution can be independent of training-set size when token counts are similar.
- Extends the analysis to BERT-1 fine-tuning on FewRel, where preserving dilution decreases with the number of labels but is stable across several numbers of examples per label.

## Method

The theoretical and toy-model analyses use binary committee machines and fully connected feedforward networks. Committee machines use binary hidden states, majority voting, and a smooth `tanh` approximation to the sign activation during back-propagation. The modified update ranks hidden units by their alignment with the desired output, updates the most relevant misaligned units, and renormalizes their weights.

For finite-error classification, the model has 105 binary input units, a 105-unit hidden layer, and $O$ output units. Each output is assigned to an equal share of the patterns. For CpL experiments, $O$ binary archetypes generate equal numbers of noisy examples through independent Bernoulli bit flips with probability $P_f$; all examples generated by an archetype receive its unique label. Dilution removes a fraction of input-to-hidden weights, and the preserving dilution is the largest dilution whose accuracy matches the undiluted model.

The NLP experiments use BERT configurations initialized randomly and trained with cross-entropy, AdamW, L2 regularization, and a linear learning-rate schedule. BERT-6 uses six transformer blocks and masked language modeling on randomly sampled Wikipedia paragraphs. APT is computed from a deterministic token-level confusion-matrix evaluation. Fine-tuning uses BERT-1, a one-block configuration initialized from the pre-training setup, on FewRel relation-classification labels.

## Experiments

### Committee machines and finite-error toy models

For restricted committee machines with 1,500 inputs and non-overlapping receptive fields, the estimated CpW is approximately 2.96 for three hidden units and 3.33 for five hidden units. A two-hidden-layer non-overlapping model gives approximately 2.99, and overlapping fully connected models with one, two, or three hidden layers give nearly identical CpW estimates. In a three-output finite-error model at CpW = 5, accuracy saturates near 0.78. For a fixed weight budget, accuracy decreases with the number of outputs, with the reported log-log fit suggesting an approximate power-law exponent of 0.9.

In the noisy-archetype model, training and test accuracy are nearly identical, fall toward zero as $P_f$ approaches 0.5, and are approximately symmetric around that noise level. Increasing the number of archetypes reduces accuracy at fixed CpW and noise. For fixed output count and noise level, the accuracy plateau is largely independent of dataset size and only slightly affected by using one, two, or three hidden layers. The maximal preserving dilution decreases as the number of archetypes increases.

### BERT pre-training

The initial BERT-6 analysis prunes the weights in transformer blocks 2-6 while excluding QKV attention layers. At 95% pruning of the remaining layers in those blocks, comparable APT is reported for datasets of 20,000 and 40,000 Wikipedia paragraphs containing similar token counts; 98% pruning substantially lowers APT. In the expanded analysis, pruning all transformer blocks, QKV layers, and the classifier head yields APT values of 0.191 and 0.250 without dilution for the 20,000- and 40,000-paragraph datasets, respectively. The same dilution configuration, 30% for all layers of block 1, 40% for QKV layers in blocks 2-6, 90% for other layers in blocks 2-6, and 20% for the classifier head, gives APT 0.191 and 0.251.

The layer-specific tolerances are uneven. QKV layers in blocks 2-6, the first transformer block, and the classifier head tolerate less pruning than the other layers. The authors interpret this as evidence that preserving accuracy depends on where weights are removed, not only on the total number of remaining weights.

### FewRel fine-tuning

Using BERT-1 on FewRel, the maximal dilution preserving undiluted accuracy decreases as the number of labels increases when there are 630 examples per label. The same pattern is reported when the number of examples per label changes from 100 to 630. In the detailed results, 64 labels retain accuracy 0.613 versus 0.613 after 20% dilution with 630 examples per label, 0.588 versus 0.587 with 300 examples, and 0.550 versus 0.549 with 100 examples. For 10 labels, 80% dilution preserves the corresponding accuracies: 0.874, 0.858, and 0.829 for 630, 300, and 100 examples per label, respectively. Each reported standard deviation is based on five independent trials.

## Limitations

The BERT evidence uses small datasets, limited BERT configurations, and a restricted set of pre-training and classification tasks. The first pruning analysis excludes some architectural components, while the expanded analysis is computationally demanding and does not establish a universal layer-wise pruning rule. The authors also note that attention layers, early blocks, and classifier heads have distinct pruning tolerances.

The toy model supports the proposed interpretation but does not reproduce the full structure or data distribution of NLP. Its archetypes, Bernoulli noise, and equal label allocation simplify the problem. The CpW comparison is also not a direct theoretical characterization of deep multi-output transformers, and the reported output-count dependence is described as a likely power law rather than a general law. Finally, the proposed CpL still requires a choice of reference accuracy, dilution scheme, label set, and training procedure, so it should be treated as an operational task-and-architecture measure rather than a universal intrinsic capacity.
