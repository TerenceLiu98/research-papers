## TL;DR

Using the January, June, and September 1980 ANES panel, MacKuen and Brown find that political environments predict individual attitude change beyond prior attitudes and individual characteristics. Neighbors’ current vote intentions better explain candidate and party evaluations, whereas neighbors’ partisan loyalties better explain changes in partisan identification. The authors interpret this contrast as evidence that social circles filter contemporary political information while also shaping political self-identification.

## Research Question

Does political context predict changes in citizens’ views, and does influence operate through stable partisan environments or the content of current political communication?

## Motivation

Cross-sectional similarities between neighbors cannot readily distinguish interpersonal influence from selection and shared characteristics. Panel observations permit examination of subsequent change, while county and named-neighbor measures expose different meanings of political context.

## Contributions

- Compares aggregate county context with respondents’ perceptions of specific neighborhood friends.
- Distinguishes current electoral preferences from stable partisan loyalties as predictors of attitude change.
- Shows that contextual associations vary with the campaign interval and the attitude being explained.
- Interprets asymmetric candidate effects through social-reality testing: peers confirm or challenge information originating in the broader campaign.

## Method

The study uses three waves of the 1980 ANES panel. County context is average Democratic or Republican presidential vote share over 1972–1980. Neighborhood context records perceived partisanship and vote intentions for up to three named neighbors. Counts are scaled from zero to one; these are perceived political environments, not independently interviewed discussion partners.

Regression outcomes are changes in candidate and party feeling thermometers. Predictors include initial feelings, context, own partisanship, ideology, demographic characteristics, and campaign information or attentiveness. Separate specifications interact county context with initial feelings. Models compare January–June, June–September, and January–September changes. Additional analyses explain change in partisan identification, rescaled to 0–100.

The lagged-attitude coefficient combines reinforcement and reequilibration; the data do not separately identify them. Joint neighborhood models compare the incremental adjusted explanatory power of neighbors’ partisanship and vote intentions.

## Experiments

These are observational panel analyses, not randomized experiments.

The identification analysis starts from an individualistic model explaining 5.1% of change variance. Neighbors’ partisanship has a January–September coefficient of 9.9 on the 0–100 scale; its influence is concentrated in June–September. Candidate effects are asymmetric: Reagan-supporting circles predict more favorable Reagan and less favorable Carter evaluations, while Carter-supporting circles do not comparably depress Reagan evaluations. The authors connect this asymmetry to the campaign’s information supply, rather than treating context as a constant independent force.

## Limitations

- Residential selection, friendship selection, and projection onto neighbors remain alternative explanations. Temporal ordering and controls do not establish causal identification.
- Only 66% name a neighborhood social contact; about one-third can identify a neighbor’s party or vote intention. Workplace and other nonresidential networks are omitted.
- County averages are coarse and can capture historical expectations rather than current discussion. Negative Democratic-county coefficients are interpreted post hoc in light of Carter’s troubled candidacy.
- Collinearity makes interaction and joint neighborhood estimates imprecise. Note 8 cautions that joint-model standard errors are around five thermometer points.
- Discussion-frequency measures do not improve the analysis. Actual conversational content and social-reality testing are inferred rather than directly observed.
- One campaign cannot establish the long-run contagion dynamics discussed in the conclusion.
