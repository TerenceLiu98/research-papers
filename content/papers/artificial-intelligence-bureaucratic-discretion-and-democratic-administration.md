## TL;DR

The article argues that replacing bureaucratic judgment with algorithmic decisions risks [[concepts/public-value-failure|Public Value Failure]] in accountability, fairness and equity, individual consideration, and reason-giving. Its proposed centaur model assigns AI an advisory role while officials retain decision authority, responsibility, and the ability to override recommendations. This is a normative institutional argument supported by illustrative cases, not a tested estimate of the effects of automation.

## Research Question

Under what conditions does transferring bureaucratic discretion to AI undermine democratic values, and how should public agencies distinguish decisions suitable for automation from those requiring human judgment?

## Motivation

Cost-saving and modernization agendas encourage governments to replace administrative personnel with AI. The article treats this as a question about the institutional foundations of democratic administration: efficiency and predictive accuracy do not by themselves establish whether a decision respects individual circumstances, can be contested, or has an accountable decision-maker. [[concepts/street-level-discretion|Street-Level Discretion]] can produce bias and inconsistency, but also provides room to reconcile general rules with particular cases.

## Contributions

- Applies the public values framework to distinguish four clusters of democratic obligations threatened by the replacement of human discretion.
- Argues that dispersed algorithmic responsibility and reliance on statistical patterns create institutionally distinct failures from biased or inconsistent human judgment.
- Develops criteria for automation based on stakes, data adequacy, moral conflict, contestability, accountability, and public participation.
- Proposes a centaur model backed by statutory human review, impact assessments, and investment in public servants' capacity to scrutinize AI.

## Method

The author uses normative institutional analysis (Section 2.4). The argument combines the Friedrich-Finer debate over administrative responsibility, Lipsky's account of frontline discretion, and the transition from street-level to system-level bureaucracy described by Bovens and Zouridis. Public values theory supplies evaluative criteria; political philosophy and prior empirical studies inform the analysis of dignity, equity, legitimacy, and responsibility. Official reports, journalism, and scholarship provide illustrative cases rather than a systematically sampled comparative dataset.

| Value cluster | Proposed failure mechanism | Recommended institutional response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accountability | Responsibility becomes dispersed among vendors, developers, agencies, and users | Assign identifiable responsible officials and enforceable oversight |
| Fairness and equity | Good aggregate performance can conceal poor treatment of atypical or minority cases | Audit bias and report performance across groups |
| Individual consideration | Recorded features omit circumstances relevant to equitable judgment | Retain human review and authority to make justified exceptions |
| Reason-giving and legitimacy | Affected people cannot obtain or contest a defensible explanation | Provide understandable reasons and meaningful human appeal |

The proposed division of labor permits automation for routine, well-structured tasks, while reserving consequential, ambiguous, or morally contested decisions for accountable officials (Section 7). [[concepts/human-oversight-of-ai|Human Oversight of AI]] must include training, override authority, and organizational encouragement to question recommendations. The discussion also warns that staffing reductions and vendor dependence can erode the expertise necessary to exercise that authority.

## Experiments

No original experiments, benchmark results, or causal effect estimates are reported. The principal evidence consists of examples and findings from cited sources:

- **Robodebt:** automated income averaging and debt notices illustrate how removing review can produce wrongful demands and undermine accountability (Section 3.2).
- **UK examination grading in 2020:** the article uses school-history-based grade adjustment to illustrate conflict between aggregate standardization and individual merit (Section 4).
- **Predictive policing and COMPAS:** the discussion illustrates feedback loops, group disparities, and the risk of substituting group profiles for individualized judgment (Section 5).
- **Countervailing evidence:** cited work on bail prediction supports potential improvements in consistency and predictive performance; other cited studies describe designs that support professional reflection rather than displace it (Sections 5-6).

The reference to 71 US legal challenges is a finding attributed to Gules-Guctas (2026), not a new case analysis conducted by this article. The centaur model and proposed safeguards are institutional recommendations whose effects are not evaluated here.

## Limitations

The cases illustrate possible mechanisms but do not establish their prevalence, identify the causal effect of replacing officials, or demonstrate that hybrid systems outperform either humans or automation alone. The claim that algorithmic decision-making is fundamentally incompatible with particular democratic obligations is the author's normative position, not a result established by comparative testing.

The article acknowledges human bias, inconsistency, and the benefits of routine automation. Its recommendations depend on officials having sufficient competence, independence, and resources to exercise meaningful judgment. Broader implications for nondemocratic settings and imported technological assumptions are discussed without a full comparative treatment. Citizen-side automation of submissions and appeals is identified as a future research question.
