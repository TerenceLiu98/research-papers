## TL;DR

In a Nairobi laboratory study, affect misattribution tasks detect more favorable reactions to coethnic cues, while standard Dictator and Public Goods Games detect no average ethnic bias. A weapon identification task also detects a smaller effect. The findings support measuring multiple dimensions of bias; they do not establish that social desirability alone explains the games’ null results.

## Research Question

Can misattribution-based tasks detect affective and threat-related ethnic biases that common economic games miss?

## Motivation

Ethnic bias can involve altruism, cooperation, dislike, or threat perception. A null result on one dimension need not imply absence of bias on others. Deliberate game choices may also be shaped by self-presentation despite monetary incentives.

## Contributions

- Adapts affect, weapon, and facial-emotion tasks to a Kenyan setting with variable literacy and computer familiarity.
- Compares these measures with economic games within the same participants.
- Distinguishes measurement sensitivity to different constructs from the ability to conceal socially disapproved responses.

## Method

Kikuyu and Luo participants recruited from Kibera and Kawangware completed games and tasks before a Kenyan presidential election. Hometowns and regions cued ethnicity without explicitly labeling ethnic groups. Task order was fixed: economic games preceded misattribution tasks, with weapon identification last.

The [[concepts/affect-misattribution-procedure|Affect Misattribution Procedure]] (AMP) presents a word prime for 200 ms, then a Chinese character for 500 ms; participants judge the character’s pleasantness. Town and region primes test ethnic associations, while universally positive and negative words provide a task check. Each version contains 30 trials. The Weapon Misidentification Task (WMT) uses name-and-hometown cues before briefly showing a man holding a weapon or similarly shaped tool. Face Anger Attribution (FAA) asks whether an ambiguous face looks happy or angry, with fast and slow presentation variants; each WMT/FAA version contains 40 trials.

Dictator Game (DG) allocations and Public Goods Game (PGG) contributions use 100 KSh endowments. Choose Your Dictator (CYD) asks participants to select a dictator, either without or with the dictator knowing their profile. Individual ethnic effects compare positive-response rates or contributions under coethnic versus noncoethnic cues; sample effects average these individual differences. CYD tests coethnic selection against 50% among those selecting a human dictator.

## Experiments

Table 1 reports 319 participants in the full sample and 182 in the final literacy-screened sample. Trials with response times at or below 200 ms are excluded, averaging 11.5% of trials per participant. Table 2 reports:

| Task | Mean effect | SE | p value |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| AMP-Town | 0.078 | 0.017 | <0.001 |
| AMP-Region | 0.130 | 0.023 | <0.001 |
| WMT | 0.027 | 0.012 | 0.029 |
| FAA-Slow | −0.002 | 0.017 | 0.907 |
| FAA-Fast | 0.023 | 0.025 | 0.351 |
| DG | −0.014 | 0.012 | 0.231 |
| PGG | 0.002 | 0.017 | 0.904 |
| CYD-Anonymous | 0.020 | 0.041 | 0.681 |
| CYD-Profiled | 0.099 | 0.041 | 0.021 |

Misattribution effects are probability differences for positive responses; DG/PGG effects are in units of 100 KSh; CYD effects are departures from 50%. AMP-Universal gives a 0.321 effect (SE 0.027), supporting feasibility of the task in this sample rather than measuring ethnic bias. The profiled CYD result suggests expectations of others’ ethnic favoritism.

## Limitations

Different constructs and differential susceptibility to self-presentation remain competing explanations. The authors explicitly reserve judgment on WMT reliability and reproducibility. FAA averages are null despite heterogeneous individual estimates. Literacy exclusions constrain generalization, fixed task order permits order effects, and geographic cues may evoke place associations as well as ethnicity. This study does not directly establish prediction of real-world discriminatory behavior or violence.

The supplied text has internal numerical discrepancies: Section 3 reports CYD-Profiled as 9.5 percentage points (z = 2.30), whereas Table 2 gives 9.9 points (z = 2.392). Section 2.4 reports 136 literacy exclusions, while Table 1’s sample sizes differ by 137. The summary retains the table values without resolving these discrepancies.
