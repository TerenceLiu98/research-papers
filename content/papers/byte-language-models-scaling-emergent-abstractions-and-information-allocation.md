## TL;DR

Flat [[concepts/byte-language-models|Byte Language Models]] trained with token-superposition and hash embeddings achieve lower bits per byte than matched subword models at the same parameter count, though not at the same training FLOPs. The models develop segmentation-like local representations without an explicit tokenizer: compressing 7 of 28 later layers to operate on approximately fourfold fewer pooled states preserves downstream performance. Byte-level prediction also concentrates uncertainty near learned structure boundaries, allowing small draft models to produce about 3.4 times as many accepted tokens as subword drafts in a teacher-forced oracle analysis.

## Research Question

Can the extra computation induced by byte sequences improve language modeling, can a standard flat Transformer recover the local abstractions supplied by tokenization, and can the resulting allocation of prediction difficulty be exploited at inference time?

## Motivation

Byte models provide a universal input space and avoid fixed tokenizer assumptions, but their sequences are much longer and lack explicit subword units. Recent tokenizer-free systems often address these costs with specialized local-global architectures. This paper instead studies whether longer sequences can be a useful computation axis and whether token-like structure emerges inside an otherwise flat Transformer.

## Contributions

- Compares byte and 49,152-vocabulary subword Transformers across parameter, data, and compute scales using bits per byte as a common loss measure.
- Shows that token-superposition training and hashed n-gram embeddings make byte models competitive while isolating a parameter-efficiency versus compute-efficiency trade-off.
- Uses a copying probe and a frozen layer intervention to identify locally aggregated byte representations that support later compressed computation.
- Characterizes byte-weighted loss distributions and relates their near-zero mass and high-loss boundaries to token grouping.
- Evaluates small same-family drafts as a proof of concept for allocating less target-model computation to predictable byte continuations.

## Method

The main models are flat hybrid-attention Transformers trained from scratch at roughly 200M, 400M, 700M, 1B, and 3B active parameters. BYTE uses a 256-entry vocabulary, wider local convolutions, hashed byte n-grams of lengths 5-8, and token-superposition training for the first 30% of updates. SUBWORD uses the cosmo2 byte-level BPE tokenizer, averaging about 4.56 UTF-8 bytes per token. IsoFLOPs fits adapt a Chinchilla-style loss law over computation per token and consumed source bytes.

For the representation analysis, a copying prompt exposes attention from generated bytes to an earlier source string. A separate frozen intervention partitions prompts into loss-defined local spans with a matched 200M model, mean-pools each span, lets a selected interval of the pretrained 1B model operate on the compressed sequence, and maps the updates back to full resolution. No parameters are retrained.

The information-allocation study measures byte-weighted bits per byte (BPB), including public byte and subword models. It compares native byte losses with losses averaged over subword-token spans. Finally, 50M and 200M drafts are compared with same-family 1B targets using teacher forcing on validation text; a verification run ends at the first draft-target disagreement.

## Experiments

### Scaling and Downstream Tasks

At the same compute-optimal parameter count, BYTE has lower fitted BPB than SUBWORD, indicating that longer sequences convert additional computation into better parameter efficiency. At the same total training FLOPs, however, SUBWORD remains better. BYTE optima allocate about 3.2 times as many source bytes per parameter. Four-pass shared-depth SUBWORD models close much of the BPB gap, while sparse MoE improves BYTE's activated-compute efficiency.

BYTE improves fine-grained tasks at every reported scale. At 1B, CUTE rises from 76.44% to 99.56%, OCRBench from 31.50% to 39.40%, and TextVQA from 47.50% to 52.36%. Across broader evaluation, BYTE is reported stronger on text tasks and roughly on par on vision-language tasks, consistent with receiving more computation per parameter.

### Emergent Local Abstractions

In copying, intermediate-layer attention repeatedly reads from a small set of source positions near local chunk boundaries, including when spaces are removed or replaced. Under the frozen intervention, BYTE recovers when compression begins sufficiently late: layers 21-27, 25% of the 28-layer model, can operate on approximately fourfold-compressed local representations while retaining near-baseline results across the tested text and vision-language tasks. The matched SUBWORD model degrades across almost all compressed intervals.

### Information Allocation and Draft Acceptance

BYTE places much more probability mass near zero loss and more mass in the high-BPB tail. For BYTE-1B, 56.6% of retained bytes have BPB below 0.1, versus 16.3% for SUBWORD-1B; 7.43% exceed 4 BPB, versus 2.35%. Averaging byte losses within subword-token spans largely closes this distribution gap, supporting the interpretation that tokenization groups difficult boundary bytes with predictable continuations.

With a 200M draft, BYTE and SUBWORD accept 9.527 and 2.831 native tokens per simulated target forward pass, respectively, a 3.366x ratio. Their accepted text coverage is nearly identical at 9.527 versus 9.523 bytes because subword tokens are longer. With a 50M draft, BYTE accepts 5.471 bytes per forward versus 5.111 for SUBWORD.

## Limitations

- The scaling and abstraction results cover the evaluated flat hybrid-Transformer family, model sizes up to 3B, and the reported training distributions; generalization to larger scales and other architectures is unresolved.
- BYTE's lower loss at a fixed parameter count requires more training computation, and SUBWORD remains better at matched total FLOPs in the main scaling comparison.
- The local spans used by the frozen intervention come from a separate smaller model rather than being inferred directly by the intervened model, and the intervention demonstrates representational sufficiency rather than an end-to-end speedup.
- The speculative-decoding experiment is an oracle, teacher-forced diagnostic on reference histories with uncapped proposal length. It does not measure generated-history quality, correction and bonus steps, drafting overhead, wall-clock latency, or realized serving speedup.
