## TL;DR

Homan and Lantis argue that ideological distance from party moderates, combined with willingness to challenge party establishments, can bring progressive Democrats and conservative Republicans together on foreign policy. A descriptive plausibility probe of six House war-powers votes under Obama and Trump finds that supporters of withdrawal or restraints on presidential force generally have more negative free-agency scores. The evidence supports a coalition-building hypothesis rather than establishing causal effects or out-of-sample predictive accuracy.

## Research Question

Which lawmakers become foreign policy free agents, and when do legislators at opposite ideological extremes cooperate despite partisan polarization?

## Motivation

Bipartisanship need not originate among moderates. Disagreement within parties and shared opposition to executive excess can open opportunities for cross-party cooperation even as the parties become more ideologically separated. [[concepts/foreign-policy-free-agency|Foreign Policy Free Agency]] connects these opportunities to individual legislators' ideology, backgrounds, and relationships with party leaders.

## Contributions

- Develops a multidimensional account of free agency involving personal motivations, factional affiliation, and opportunities created by polarization.
- Constructs a composite index from two DW-NOMINATE dimensions to identify potential free agents across parties.
- Compares voting blocs in the 112th and 116th Congresses, combining score averages with qualitative accounts of legislative maneuvering.

## Method

The first DW-NOMINATE dimension measures liberal–conservative position. The authors interpret negative second-dimension scores as anti-establishment orientation or unwillingness to compromise with party leadership. They reverse the sign of Republican first-dimension scores and add the second dimension; Democratic scores are added without that reversal. Scores nearer −2 indicate stronger potential free agency. This equal-weight sum measures a proposed propensity, not observed coalition membership.

The mixed-method design compares yes/no bloc averages, party averages, and the two component scores, using Voteview data and accounts of bills, floor debates, and speeches. Cases concern Afghanistan and Libya under Obama and Yemen and Iran under Trump. It is explicitly a plausibility probe, with purposively selected high-profile cases.

Under Trump, only 18, 11, and 3 Republicans supported the three respective measures in Table 1. Democratic support was nearly unanimous, providing less within-party discrimination. The Republican votes were unnecessary for passage, so the cases demonstrate alignment without establishing pivotal influence.

Under Obama, progressive Democrats joined conservative Republicans in supporting withdrawal resolutions. Boehner's alternative demanded an explanation of the Libya operation without requiring withdrawal: legislators opposing this weaker measure had more negative scores. Thus free agency does not imply voting yes on every measure challenging a president. Qualitative accounts show how leadership alternatives can redirect an emerging coalition.

Appendix Table 5 describes historical component and composite averages. Table 6 supplies domestic-vote comparisons; the authors suggest foreign-policy distinctiveness, but do not establish it through a systematic comparative test.

## Limitations

- Selected votes and group means do not isolate ideology, anti-establishment orientation, or other motivations causally. No held-out prediction benchmark is reported.
- Interpreting the second DW-NOMINATE dimension as anti-establishment sentiment is a substantive assumption; the paper acknowledges its different historical interpretations. The composite also imposes equal weighting.
- Some supporting blocs are extremely small, and near-unanimous Democratic votes under Trump limit discrimination. Evidence of voting together does not itself establish policy influence.
- Generalization to trade, immigration, other legislatures, or future Congresses remains open. The authors call for broader quantitative analysis.
