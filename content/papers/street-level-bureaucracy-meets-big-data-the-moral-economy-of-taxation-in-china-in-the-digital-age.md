## TL;DR

Ethnographic research in a Chinese county tax bureau argues that digitalization reconstitutes discretion: officials shift from intrusive information gathering to negotiating the interpretation of algorithmically flagged discrepancies. Revenue targets and sympathy for local small businesses support a “good enough” approach to enforcement. The paper connects [[concepts/street-level-discretion|Street-Level Discretion]] with the [[concepts/moral-economy|Moral Economy]] of taxation.

## Research Question

How does big-data taxation change frontline officials’ knowledge, discretion, and moral judgments, and why do officials sometimes welcome these changes?

## Motivation

Accounts emphasizing either automation-driven displacement or resistance overlook how technology creates new sites of judgment. Taxation makes this issue visible because information extraction, enforcement, fairness, and state legitimacy intersect in encounters with taxpayers.

## Contributions

- Proposes a transformation thesis: discretion moves between stages of administration rather than simply surviving or disappearing.
- Traces a shift from individualized knowledge collected through repeated visits to industry-relative knowledge generated by statistical models.
- Explains negotiated enforcement through revenue-target incentives and officials’ changing identification with local society under administrative centralization.

## Method

The study combines interviews with tax collectors, inspectors, bureau leaders, and business owners; participant observation; and bureau archives in anonymized County M, a coastal Chinese county with a vibrant small-enterprise economy and relatively early digital adoption. Fieldwork occurred in May–July 2018, June–July 2019, and June–August 2023. Retrospective interviews reconstruct roughly three decades of change; this is not three decades of continuous observation. The author shadowed four taxpayer interviews and followed the workflow from information gathering through risk interpretation and negotiation.

## Experiments

This is a qualitative case study, not an experiment. Its principal evidence is historical comparison, observed interactions, interview accounts, and administrative records.

| Evidence | Reported finding |
| --- | --- |
| Administrative workload | Enterprises grew from about 2,000 to 12,000 over two decades, while frontline tax personnel increased by only two to 40. |
| Inspection capacity | More than 100 inspectors handled roughly 1,000 cases annually in the 1990s; eight handled 23 cases in 2015. Inspection authority shifted to the city level around 2018–2019. |
| Risk assessment | A model uses 20 indicators combining declarations, invoices, and other data with historical and industry comparisons. Scores guide warnings, interviews, and inspection. |
| Negotiation | Officials invite explanations and selective admissions that resolve anomalies; they can demand more evidence or escalate, but commonly seek an acceptable settlement. |
| Revenue targets | Table 1 reports fulfillment of 100.6%, 100.1%, 101.2%, 100.6%, 100.8%, 102.5%, 106.8%, and 106.8% for 2014–2021. |

Digital mediation reduces burdensome and conflictual encounters while preserving uncertainty: declarations remain partly self-reported and computational processes are opaque to many officials. The paper interprets this combination as permitting negotiated resolution rather than mechanically determined liabilities. Revenue targets encourage adequate collection without excessive overachievement that raises future targets. Sympathy shifts from particular personal relationships toward local small businesses as a category, reinforced by nonlocal leadership appointments and preferential treatment of externally attracted firms.

## Limitations

- One early-adopting county with many small enterprises supports a situated mechanism account, not a national causal estimate or a universal claim about automation.
- Retrospective accounts and four observed negotiation sessions cannot establish the prevalence of each practice across jurisdictions. The supplied text does not specify a total interview sample.
- Technology, inspection centralization, career structures, and local economic change evolve together; the design does not isolate their independent causal effects.
- Leniency and reduced conflict do not themselves establish legal consistency, distributive fairness, or improved compliance. The conclusion explicitly raises oversight of officials’ interpretive power.
- The prose gives a minimum target-fulfillment rate of 100.6%, but Table 1 includes 100.1%; the table values are retained here. Risk-score boundaries also overlap in the source, so exact threshold inclusion should not be inferred.
