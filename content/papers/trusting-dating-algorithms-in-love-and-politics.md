## TL;DR

In a weighted analysis of 839 recent U.S. online daters, belief in matchmaking algorithms was associated with less political profile screening among non-love seekers but more screening among long-term relationship seekers and balanced users. Emotional associations also differed across groups: trust was directly associated with less disappointment among long-term seekers, while their screening was associated with worse emotions. These are cross-sectional associations, not demonstrated causal effects.

## Research Question

How do beliefs about matchmaking algorithms relate to political partner-vetting and emotional experience, and do mixed dating motivations moderate these relationships?

## Motivation

Algorithmic recommendations and users' own political screening may complement or substitute for one another. Treating all daters as having the same relationship goals can obscure these differences. Expectancy violations offer a possible explanation for disappointment when trusted matching systems fail to deliver compatible partners.

## Contributions

- Connects algorithmic belief, political screening, and excitement and disappointment within one survey analysis.
- Uses joint motivation profiles rather than a single casual-versus-serious dating distinction.
- Shows that the proposed negative association between algorithmic belief and screening holds only among non-love seekers.

## Method

The study uses Pew Research Center's July 2022 American Trends Panel Wave 111 survey. Of 6,034 respondents, 951 had dated online during the previous year; complete-case selection yielded 839. K-means clustering of four three-point motivation items, with the elbow method selecting three clusters, identified non-love seekers (251), long-term relationship or LTR sticklers (313), and balanced users (275).

Algorithmic belief is a three-category response about whether algorithms could determine if two people will eventually fall in love. Political partner-vetting is operationalized as the importance of political affiliation appearing in profiles, on a four-point scale. Excitement and disappointment are separate three-point items about the preceding year. Thus, the screening measure captures stated importance rather than observed screening activity.

Weighted grouped regressions include demographic controls, internet use, Democratic partisan leaning, and ideological distance from the scale midpoint. Full-sample interaction models compare groups, and mediation analyses use 1,000 bootstrap samples.

## Experiments

This is an observational survey study, with no randomized experiment. Tables 3–4 report the following unstandardized coefficients:

| Association | Non-love seekers | LTR sticklers | Balanced users |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Algorithmic belief → vetting | −0.31, p < .001 | 0.16, p = .023 | 0.16, p = .023 |
| Vetting → excitement | 0.12, p = .003 | −0.06, p = .032 | 0.01, nonsignificant |
| Vetting → disappointment | 0.05, nonsignificant | 0.14, p < .001 | −0.02, nonsignificant |
| Algorithmic belief → excitement, controlling for vetting | 0.07, nonsignificant | 0.02, nonsignificant | 0.09, p = .043 |
| Algorithmic belief → disappointment, controlling for vetting | 0.09, nonsignificant | −0.18, p < .001 | −0.05, nonsignificant |

The authors report negative indirect associations with excitement through vetting for non-love seekers and LTR sticklers, and a positive indirect association with disappointment for LTR sticklers. The latter coexists with a negative direct association. Interaction models support differences in the belief–vetting relationship between non-love seekers and the other two groups. Democratic leaning and ideological extremity are positively associated with vetting in all groups.

## Limitations

Cross-sectional data cannot establish temporal order or causal mediation. Single-item measures narrowly represent trust, screening, and emotion; sexual orientation was unavailable as a control. Platform-specific practices and additional emotional outcomes are not modeled. A complete-case subset of recent online daters should not be equated with the entire U.S. population merely because the source survey is nationally representative.

The supplied Markdown reports several mediation point estimates outside their accompanying confidence intervals, including −0.04 with [−0.02, −0.01]. Exact mediation intervals therefore require checking against the publication; they are not reproduced as reliable estimates here. Proposed delegation, cognitive dissonance, and expectancy-violation mechanisms remain interpretations rather than directly measured processes.
