## TL;DR

Renwick argues that Hungary’s fully competitive transition settlement and Poland’s initially restricted settlement reflected not only negotiating circumstances in 1989, but also opposition frames developed during the preceding decade. Hungarian dissidents prioritized reform of state institutions; Solidarity prioritized independent social organization and trade-union legalization. These frames shaped which reforms actors could envisage and what concessions regimes expected to make.

## Research Question

Why did two negotiated transitions from communism produce different degrees of immediate democratization, and how did earlier understandings of feasible change shape the negotiations?

## Motivation

Poland’s February–April 1989 negotiations reserved 65% of Sejm seats for the communists and their allies, while opening the remaining 35% and all 100 Senate seats to competition. Hungary’s June–September negotiations provided for competition for every parliamentary seat. Timing, reform-communist strength, and opposition bargaining power explain important differences, but Hungarian demands for institutional pluralism already exceeded Polish demands before the Polish elections and the subsequent clarification of Soviet tolerance.

## Contributions

- Adds [[concepts/medium-term-political-frames|medium-term political frames]] to explanations of negotiated democratization.
- Distinguishes state pluralism, centered on representative state institutions, from societal corporatism, centered on autonomous interest organizations recognized by the state.
- Reconstructs frame formation through both structural opportunities and contingencies, including historical precedents and the influence of particular intellectuals.
- Argues that historical reconstruction complements rational-choice models by explaining how actors come to perceive their available options.

## Method

The article develops a comparative historical explanation using opposition programmes, underground publications, contemporary reporting, negotiation records, secondary scholarship, and an interview with János Kis. It examines three immediate-context explanations before tracing opposition discourse and its origins in the late 1970s and 1980s.

The Helsinki human-rights commitments and Solidarity’s rise and suppression helped unsettle earlier dissident frameworks. In Hungary, the Beszélő circle’s 1982 programme and 1987 Social Contract elaborated institutional reform and a longer-term parliamentary-democratic goal. Expanding opportunities in 1988 brought that established goal onto the immediate agenda. Polish mainstream opposition statements instead concentrated on Solidarity’s legalization, with less elaboration of subsequent institutional change. Both perspectives existed in both countries; the claim concerns their relative influence.

Regime reformism and opposition–society relations helped shape these frames. Solidarity’s mass constituency supported and constrained its union-centered agenda; Hungarian dissidents lacked comparable social roots. Contingencies also mattered: Poland’s Znak parliamentary group offered a cautionary precedent for Polish dissidents, while Kis saw limited Catholic representation as evidence that institutional reform was possible. Kis’s influence through Beszélő helped institutional reform prevail despite competing views within Hungary’s opposition.

## Experiments

This is a historical comparative study, with no experimental intervention or statistical effect estimate. Its main evidence includes:

- **Pre-negotiation divergence:** Hungarian demands for competitive elections preceded the developments between the two countries’ round tables, limiting a timing-only explanation.
- **Programmatic continuity:** Hungarian institutional proposals in 1982 and 1987 contrast with Polish statements in 1987–88 emphasizing trade-union pluralism.
- **Different electoral experiences:** In Hungary’s 1985 elections, 71 unofficial candidates reached the ballot and 25 won seats, reinforcing perceptions of feasible institutional engagement. No anti-regime candidate reached the Polish ballot.
- **Negotiated outcomes:** Hungary accepted fully competitive parliamentary elections and a relatively weak presidency; Poland initially combined restricted parliamentary competition with a presidency intended to preserve communist executive influence. Solidarity subsequently won all available Sejm seats and 99 of 100 Senate seats in June 1989.

## Limitations

The argument does not establish that Solidarity could have obtained full democratization simply by bargaining harder. Frames helped constitute the negotiating environment, including regime expectations, rather than merely obscuring fixed opportunities. Short-term circumstances and structural differences remain part of the explanation. The two-case historical account does not isolate a numerical frame effect or establish general applicability across transitions. The discussion of contingent frame formation is illustrative rather than exhaustive.
