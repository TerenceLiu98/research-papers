## TL;DR

This special-issue introduction updates [[concepts/cleavage-theory|cleavage theory]] for knowledge societies through three puzzles: durable political divisions without mass membership organisations, cleavage formation amid party fragmentation, and the indirect mobilisation of education-based structural divides. The authors argue that informal social closure, collective identities, and competition between broader party blocs can sustain new alignments, while leaving their scope and long-term stability open to empirical investigation.

## Research Question

How can contemporary political divisions qualify as cleavages when their organisational foundations, party representation, and identity categories differ from the classic class and religious cases?

## Motivation

Technological change, educational expansion, occupational restructuring, urban concentration, and changing gender relations jointly reshape political constituencies. Education increasingly distinguishes new-left and far-right electorates, but statistical prediction alone does not explain how structural positions become shared identities and durable political commitments. Declining unions and church organisations also make older models of organisational encapsulation insufficient.

## Contributions

- Organises contemporary cleavage research around three theoretical puzzles and maps special-issue contributions onto them.
- Connects education-centred knowledge-society change to informal social networks, collective identities, and civil-society mobilisation.
- Proposes looking beyond challenger parties and historical party families to contemporary blocs, distinguishing competition within a cleavage pole from movement across poles.
- Reconsiders stylised historical benchmarks: older working-class constituencies were heterogeneous and sometimes represented by competing parties.

## Method

The article offers a conceptual synthesis and research agenda, comparing classic cleavage scholarship with contemporary studies and contributions to the special issue. It presents no original statistical design or experimental intervention.

Its framework joins three elements: structural divisions; identities, values, and interests; and social or political organisation. Knowledge-society transformation creates mobilisation potentials, while political actors shape their expression.

For the first puzzle, educationally homogeneous networks, residential and workplace segregation, collective identities, and loosely organised movements are candidate stabilising mechanisms. For the second, party-bloc analysis can reveal durable boundaries beneath party-level volatility, although classification by current alignment rather than historical origins remains unresolved. For the third, education and occupation need not appear literally in voters’ self-descriptions: identities organised around place, nation, lifestyles, or recognition can mediate their political expression.

## Experiments

There are no original experiments. The reviewed evidence includes Hooghe and Marks (2025), reported as finding that education structures GAL and TAN voting as strongly as class structured social-democratic support in the mid-twentieth century. The introduction also stresses that the new cleavage remains electorally smaller than the postwar class cleavage when measured strictly through GAL and TAN parties. These are distinct comparisons of structural association and electoral reach, not contradictory findings.

Other special-issue contributions examine educational fields, education–generation–gender interactions, civil society, mainstream-right/far-right competition, and bloc volatility. The article synthesises these studies rather than providing a pooled effect estimate.

## Limitations

The single-cleavage interpretation remains contested: environmental and nationalist mobilisation may represent distinct processes, and issue competition remains multidimensional. An emerging cleavage need not organise most voters or most issues. Informal closure and collective identity are proposed mechanisms whose comparative importance requires further investigation.

How to classify mainstream parties and parties spanning multiple cleavages remains unsettled. The synthesis centres on advanced Western democracies and does not establish universal applicability or a causal estimate of education’s political effect. Whether these conflicts become democratically pacified or destabilise liberal democracy is an open question.
