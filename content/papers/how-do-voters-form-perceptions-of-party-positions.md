## TL;DR

Two randomized survey experiments show that emphasizing one of a fictional party’s contrasting policy stances shifts its perceived broader ideology. Contrasts between emphasis conditions reach about 0.8 points for economic ideology and 1.1 points for sociocultural ideology on 0–10 scales. The economic tax-emphasis effect against the two-short-statements baseline is not statistically significant.

## Research Question

Can changing issue emphasis shift perceived party ideology without reversing underlying policy directions?

## Motivation

Policy reversals can damage credibility. Emphasis offers another route to perceived repositioning if voters construct broad ideology as a weighted average of specific stances. Experiments help separate this process from prior partisan beliefs and top-down inference from ideology to issues.

## Contributions

- Randomizes emphasis in messages about hypothetical parties, avoiding established party reputations.
- Tests economic and sociocultural dimensions in two national settings.
- Uses mediation analysis to distinguish broad perception changes from changes in perceived issue stances.

## Method

Respondents read one progressive and one conservative issue statement. Each independently appears in a short version of roughly ten words or a long version of roughly sixty words, yielding four conditions; statement order is randomized. The short version is the first sentence of the longer message, which adds justification and policy detail. Two short statements provide the reference condition.

Respondents first locate the party on a broader 0–10 dimension, then rate its underlying issue positions in randomized question order. Mediation models use these issue perceptions as potential mediators. The randomized treatment identifies message effects; interpreting a direct effect as pure emphasis additionally depends on mediation assumptions and the content added by longer messages.

## Experiments

| Study | Sample | Issue contrast | Reported dimensional result |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Germany, November 2020; online quota sample, N = 3,150 | Expanded pensions and elderly care versus income-tax cuts | Economic placement means range from 3.95 to 4.77; outcome SD = 2.32 |
| II | Austria, AUTNES online panel; quota sample, N = 3,024 | Expanded LGBT rights versus reduced refugee numbers | Sociocultural placement differs by about 1.1 points across emphasis conditions; outcome SD = 2.33 |

Respondents recognize the contrasting directions: mean issue ratings are 2.76 for pensions and 6.63 for tax cuts, and 3.53 for LGBT rights and 7.34 for refugees. Higher values indicate the conservative direction.

Longer pension messages shift economic placement leftward, largely through the estimated direct pathway. Longer tax messages produce a small positive effect against the short-message baseline that misses conventional significance thresholds. In Study II, emphasizing refugee restrictions shifts perceptions rightward, while emphasizing LGBT rights shifts them leftward; indirect estimates through issue perceptions are close to zero. Some issue-level perceptions nevertheless change, especially toward more extreme positions in Study I (Results; Figures 3–6).

## Limitations

- Fictional parties remove prior reputations and partisan cues; results do not directly establish equivalent effects for familiar parties in campaigns.
- Longer messages add substantive detail, and some underlying issue perceptions change. This is not a perfectly content-invariant emphasis manipulation.
- Online quota samples in two countries limit generalization to other political contexts.
- Media and rival-party agenda effects are implications, not directly tested treatments. Persistence and electoral consequences are not established.
- The relative prevalence of bottom-up aggregation and top-down ideological inference remains unresolved. Referenced supplementary mediation tables are absent from the supplied Markdown.
