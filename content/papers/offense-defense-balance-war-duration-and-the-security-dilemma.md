## TL;DR

Across four historical codings of the systemic offense–defense balance, Nilsson finds no association with interstate war duration significant at the .05 level. This challenges the assumption that offensive advantage yields quick wars. The proposed implications for arms racing and preemption depend on leaders recognizing the risk of prolonged warfare; those behavioral implications are theoretical rather than directly estimated.

## Research Question

Does systemic offense dominance shorten wars, and how should evidence about war duration change accounts of the security dilemma?

## Motivation

Offense–defense theory links easy conquest to incentives for aggression and preventive or preemptive action. Its expectation of short, decisive wars supplies a crucial intermediate premise. Yet offensive technology can also enable counterattacks, while strategic depth allows defenders to absorb an initial assault. Tactical advantage need not translate into rapid strategic victory.

## Contributions

- Tests the war-duration premise using four competing historical classifications rather than relying on a single coding.
- Separates observed war duration from beliefs about duration in the theoretical explanation of security competition.
- Reinterprets Jervis’s four security environments by adding the accuracy of duration beliefs, yielding eight analytical scenarios.

## Method

The study adds systemic balance measures from Adams, Quester, Van Evera, and Jervis to Bennett and Stam’s updated interstate-war duration model for wars beginning in 1817–1992. Duration is measured in months. Weibull accelerated failure-time models accommodate annually varying covariates; positive coefficients indicate longer duration. Higher balance scores indicate movement toward defense dominance, with intermediate categories in the Quester and Van Evera measures.

Controls include strategy combinations, terrain and its interaction with strategy, relative capabilities, personnel, population, military quality, surprise, issue salience, repression, democracy, previous disputes, and the number of participating states. A Cox proportional hazards specification checks robustness. The subsequent game illustrations change the payoffs associated with beliefs about rapid victory; they do not estimate actual leaders’ preferences.

## Experiments

These are observational duration analyses, not randomized experiments. Table 1 reports:

| Balance coding | Wars | AFT coefficient (SE) | Interpretation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Adams | 80 | −0.253 (0.349) | Not significant at .05 |
| Quester | 73 | −0.335 (0.197) | Significant only at .10; direction opposite to the short-offensive-war prediction |
| Van Evera | 80 | −0.173 (0.233) | Not significant at .05 |
| Jervis | 63 | 0.265 (0.322) | Predicted direction, but reported p = .41 |

No balance measure reaches .05 significance. In the Cox check, even Quester’s measure fails to reach .10. Dropping strategy controls reportedly does not materially change the results. These findings undermine the proposed general association; they do not establish an exactly zero effect.

The theoretical extension argues that accurate expectations of costly, potentially long wars reduce incentives to react to small capability changes or strike first relative to Jervis’s offense-dominant scenario. Defense dominance likewise provides less reassurance than that framework assumes. Adequate defensive capability and strategic depth remain important.

## Limitations

- The analysis concerns systemic historical classifications, not dyadic offense–defense balances. Coding disagreements and differing temporal coverage yield different samples.
- Applying classifications developed for particular settings, including European great powers, to broader interstate-war samples constrains interpretation.
- Observational estimates and nonsignificance do not prove that technology has no causal effect on duration.
- Beliefs, arms racing, and security-dilemma severity are not directly tested. The behavioral conclusions require sufficiently accurate beliefs and do not rule out aggression for other reasons.
- The supplied text contains an inconsistent conversion of the Quester AFT coefficient into a hazard ratio. The coefficient is retained here without adopting that conversion.
