## TL;DR

A survey of 141 developmental researchers and adjacent professionals, supplemented by 15 interviews, identifies provisional priorities for adolescent AI research: severe harms, literacy, relational use, information and identity, and overreliance. Respondents favor complementary research methods and structural protections through regulation and developmentally informed design, alongside literacy education. These are professional judgments about priorities, not estimates of chatbot effects on youth.

## Research Question

Which adolescent AI use cases merit research priority, which questions and methods should guide the field, what obstructs research, and which protections do professionals recommend while evidence develops?

## Motivation

Rapid changes in AI products and youth practices outpace research and publication. Aggregate screen-time measures obscure distinct motivations, design features, and developmental functions. The study seeks an initial shared agenda that can coordinate research without assuming all uses have the same consequences.

## Contributions

- Maps professional priorities across 20 youth-informed AI use cases.
- Integrates quantitative ratings and qualitative themes to identify methodological needs and institutional barriers.
- Documents support for built-in safeguards, youth-centered regulation, and multidimensional literacy.
- Distinguishes acute-harm triage from the need to investigate subtle, long-term developmental changes.

## Method

The convergent mixed-methods design combines an online survey with open-ended questions and semi-structured interviews. Recruitment in fall 2025 and early spring 2026 contacted over 800 researchers, laboratories, and organizations and used relevant listservs. After duplicate removal, the survey included 141 adults, of whom 127 identified as active researchers. A purposive interview sample sought variation in roles, disciplines, and research interests; 15 of 27 invitees participated.

Six undergraduate advisors aged 19–20 helped generate and refine use cases, with additional teen input from a focus group of four and an emailed survey of twelve. Respondents rated research importance from 1 to 4. Researchers selected barriers from a checklist; all respondents could select their top five guardrails from twelve options. Checklist denominators were 114 for barriers, 124 for guardrails, and 111 for marginalized-youth priorities.

The authors used descriptive statistics and reflexive thematic analysis, integrating evidence at interpretation. Themes were constructed through iterative team interpretation rather than a fixed coding framework. The authors explicitly acknowledge their disciplinary perspectives and combine results with discussion of prior literature.

## Experiments

This is an expert-perspective study, with no randomized intervention or direct assessment of adolescent outcomes. Selected findings from Table 2 and Sections 3.2–3.3 are:

| Research priority | Mean importance (SD), 1–4 |
| --- | --- |
| Responses to self-harm, eating disorders, or crisis disclosures | 3.85 (0.38) |
| Mental health support | 3.76 (0.45) |
| AI literacy and fact-checking | 3.69 (0.56) |
| AI-generated misinformation and political content | 3.68 (0.60) |
| Companionship and friendship | 3.65 (0.58) |

All twenty use cases received majority endorsement as important or extremely important; mean ratings ranged from 2.61 to 3.85. Small differences should not be read as a precise ranking. Interviews highlighted tensions between prioritizing acute harm and studying less visible changes in agency, social reciprocity, identity, and independent skills.

Among 114 barrier respondents, 87 identified rapid technological change, 65 limited data access or industry partnerships, and 64 limited platform/model transparency. Funding shortages and academic incentives were also prominent. Respondents advocated combining behavioral records, self-reports, longitudinal research, experiments, and participatory designs according to the question. Shared measurement standards, repositories, consortia, and multisite cohorts are particularly the authors’ infrastructure synthesis, rather than uniformly expressed participant demands.

Among 124 guardrail respondents, built-in safeguards received 97 endorsements (78.2%), government regulation 94 (75.8%), and school AI literacy 91 (73.4%). Qualitative responses treated literacy as complementary to institutional protections and emphasized adolescent involvement in design. Respondents differed in their overall urgency and optimism about AI, and some cautioned against blanket bans that might remove beneficial uses.

## Limitations

Nonrepresentative recruitment reflects the authors’ networks and disproportionately represents U.S. respondents, psychology, and academia. The finite use-case list constrains what can be prioritized despite youth consultation. Item nonresponse changes denominators. Professional agreement establishes neither harm prevalence nor causal effects nor the effectiveness of proposed safeguards. Findings provide a starting point for consensus-building rather than representative field-wide consensus. Anonymized data are available by request subject to IRB approval and a data-use agreement.
