## TL;DR
Provincial relative intergenerational mobility is positively associated with desired family size in China. Cai and Xie connect this pattern to optimism about future status and social conditions, with stronger associations among disadvantaged respondents and men. The outcome measures fertility desires rather than subsequent births.

## Research Question
Does regional openness to intergenerational mobility predict fertility desires, through which subjective attitudes, and with what socioeconomic and gender differences?

## Motivation
Under [[child-centered-familism|Child-Centered Familism]], expectations about children's future status can shape perceived parenting costs. Regional opportunity structures may therefore influence fertility desires beyond individual income or personal mobility experiences.

## Contributions
- Treats mobility as a regional opportunity structure, extending studies comparing mobile and nonmobile individuals.
- Links fertility desires to future status expectations, perceived fairness, trust, and happiness.
- Examines socioeconomic and gender heterogeneity and historical instruments for regional mobility.

## Method
The analysis uses CGSS waves from 2010, 2012, 2013, 2015, 2017, and 2018: 23,923 respondents aged 18–44 across 29 mainland provincial units. Desired children without policy restrictions are grouped into zero or one, two, and three or more, coded 1–3.

[[relative-intergenerational-mobility|Relative Intergenerational Mobility]] is measured as one minus the provincial rank–rank slope between current subjective status and family status at age 14, then standardized. Mobility estimation pools waves, including 2011, and uses nonmigrants across counties aged at least 30 and born after 1964; ranks are adjusted within age groups.

Ordered logistic and linear regressions include survey-year effects, individual characteristics, origin and destination status categories, provincial economic controls, and absolute upward mobility. Additional specifications control existing sons and daughters and provincial fertility rates in 2000 and 2010. Weights equalize wave totals; standard errors cluster by province–birth cohort. Two-stage least squares uses Qing-era jinshi density and the provincial share of counties reaching the national average jinshi pass rate as separate instruments.

## Experiments
These are observational survey analyses, not randomized experiments.

| Analysis | Reported result |
|---|---|
| Fully adjusted ordered logit, Table 2 | Odds ratio 1.164 per SD of mobility, p < 0.05 |
| Fully adjusted OLS, Table 2 | 0.0372 (SE 0.0168), N = 23,923 |
| OLS without fertility controls | 0.0494 (SE 0.0156) |
| Joint mediation, Table 3 | Five attitudes account for 10.71% of the baseline coefficient |
| IV: jinshi density, Table 4 | 0.103 (SE 0.0175), N = 19,980 |
| IV: county share, Table 4 | 0.313 (SE 0.0649), N = 19,980 |

Linear coefficients refer to the coded fertility category score, not an unrestricted count of children. Expected future status individually accounts for 5.95% of the baseline association, fairness 3.45%, trust 3.02%, and happiness 2.33%; these shares are not additive. Security perception is not supported as a mediator.

Associations are stronger among lower-income respondents, with no significantly negative association among higher-income groups. Education patterns are less uniform by gender. Men show larger coefficients than women, particularly for desired sons. The authors report robustness to alternative outcome categories, Poisson models, weights, mobility constructions, and absolute-mobility controls; detailed checks are available from the authors rather than fully displayed.

## Limitations
- Pooled spatial differences do not establish effects of changes in mobility over time or demonstrate increased births.
- Subjective status and retrospective family ratings introduce measurement uncertainty; expectations about children's own prospects are not measured directly.
- Historical instruments require exclusion assumptions; larger IV coefficients alone do not establish that measurement-error attenuation explains the OLS–IV gap.
- The outcome combines childlessness with one-child preferences, limiting conclusions about voluntary childlessness.
- Cultural scope and alternative fertility motivations constrain generalization beyond this setting. The supplied Table 1 repeats 27.28% for two categories, producing inconsistent totals; those percentages are not reproduced here.
