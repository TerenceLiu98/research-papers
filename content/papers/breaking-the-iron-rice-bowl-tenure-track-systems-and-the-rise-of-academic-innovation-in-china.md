## TL;DR

Using staggered tenure-track adoption across Chinese economics departments, the paper estimates that internationally trained faculty hired after reform published 0.135 more articles per year in high-quality journals during their probationary period, roughly double the pre-reform mean. Recruitment changes and higher pay and research funding explain part of the difference; a productivity premium that rises before tenure and fades afterward, together with survey evidence, is consistent with evaluation pressure as another mechanism. The design does not fully separate these bundled mechanisms or evaluate teaching, service, or student outcomes.

## Research Question

How did replacing permanent-at-hire university employment with a tenure-track system affect research productivity in Chinese economics departments, and how much of any change can be attributed to recruitment, selective retention, performance pressure, and financial incentives?

## Motivation

Tenure track combines temporary employment, formal evaluation, clearer promotion rules, higher compensation, and research support. Its productivity effect is therefore theoretically ambiguous: stronger incentives may raise effort and attract promising researchers, while insecurity may impair performance. Evidence is difficult to obtain because institutions rarely document reform timing alongside long-run faculty publication histories. China's staggered transition from the public-sector *bianzhi* system provides variation across departments and hiring cohorts.

## Contributions

- Builds a hand-collected faculty panel linked to EconLit and documents reform timing in Chinese economics departments.
- Compares post-reform and pre-reform hiring cohorts using event-study and staggered difference-in-differences designs, with separate estimates for international- and domestic-PhD faculty.
- Examines recruitment composition, survival selection, career-stage productivity, perceived pressure, salary and funding, collaboration, research topics, authorship, and incumbent spillovers.
- Quantifies the reform's contribution to the growth of high-quality economics publications in China while distinguishing publication quantity from journal-impact-weighted output.

## Method

The study collects records for nearly 2,000 faculty members from 46 economics-related departments or schools that adopted tenure track during 2004-2018. Faculty biographies supply hiring year, education, gender, and prior experience; EconLit supplies annual publications, journal tier, impact, and coauthorship. The main individual-level regressions use 1,501 faculty members and 10,064 observations during the first six years after hiring, including 467 international-PhD and 1,034 domestic-PhD faculty.

Treatment is defined by whether a faculty member's hiring cohort entered a department after its reform. Nonparametric and parametric event studies include department, calendar-year, hiring-cohort, and career-tenure fixed effects. Staggered-DID models summarize average effects. Standard errors are clustered by department. Because early reforms primarily applied to overseas-trained hires, domestic-PhD faculty provide a substantively useful comparison group but are not a perfectly randomized control.

Robustness analyses use alternative estimators for heterogeneous effects under staggered adoption, including [[concepts/stacked-event-studies|Stacked Event Studies]], nonlinear count models, different event windows, regression-discontinuity plots around reform cohorts, university-level clustering, department-year fixed effects, and department-cohort fixed effects. Pre-trend plots and adoption-timing tests support the design but cannot rule out all time-varying faculty-specific confounding.

Mechanism analyses compare pre-hire characteristics and potentially selected samples, trace output across career stages, survey perceived pressure, and control for department-reported salary and research-funding differences. These exercises decompose associations rather than experimentally isolating each component of the reform bundle.

## Experiments

Department-level publication gains emerge after reform and grow over time. At the faculty level, the parametric event study estimates 0.071 additional annual top-three-tier publications for the full sample and 0.135 for international-PhD faculty; the latter is approximately a 100% increase over their pre-treatment mean of 0.135. The corresponding domestic-PhD estimate is 0.007 and statistically insignificant. For international-PhD faculty, the estimates are 0.113 additional SSCI publications and 1.052 additional SJR-weighted publication units. Alternative staggered-treatment estimators and count models retain the main pattern.

The quality results do not indicate a quantity-quality tradeoff. Gains are concentrated in top-one-tier and tier-two journals, while effects weaken for tier-three and broad SSCI outlets. International-PhD faculty also publish more in the top three Chinese-language journals, but not in the wider top-nine or CSSCI groups.

Recruitment changes after reform: new hires are more often male, North American trained, and in possession of high-quality revise-and-resubmit papers. The study reports no significant shifts in doctoral-program rank, prior publication totals, or pre-hire coauthor-network strength. Adding observed recruitment characteristics reduces the baseline coefficient by about 17-21% in the paper's preferred decomposition; a broader controlled specification reduces it by about 23%.

Selective retention does not appear to explain the result. Only 10 of 44 surveyed reformed departments reported dismissals after failed tenure review, generally affecting less than 10% of faculty. Cohort sizes and turnover do not change visibly, and estimates remain similar after excluding faculty who had completed probation or departments reporting dismissals.

The productivity premium for international-PhD hires rises during probation, peaks near the tenure decision, and falls sharply afterward. No comparable pattern appears among domestic-PhD faculty. Survey respondents in both personnel systems also perceive greater pre-promotion pressure under tenure track and little pressure difference after promotion. This timing supports the pressure mechanism, though it does not identify it independently of other changing incentives.

Tenure-track salaries average 144,500 RMB more per year and research funding 34,800 RMB more than under the traditional system. Controlling for these differences accounts for about 22-27% of the estimated productivity increase; after also accounting for observed recruitment characteristics, 52-61% remains unexplained. Output gains are concentrated in solo work and teams with one or two coauthors, especially external collaborators, empirical and China-focused research, and first-authored or alphabetically ordered papers.

Spillover estimates for incumbent faculty are modestly positive but not robust across specifications. Counterfactual calculations attribute about 20% of the rise in per-capita top-three-tier publications among international-PhD faculty and 12% among all sampled faculty to direct reform effects. A separate calculation values the estimated 0.135-publication gain at 13,500 RMB annually using common publication bonuses, about 10% of the salary premium; it excludes broader faculty contributions and is not a welfare analysis.

## Limitations

- Reform timing was not randomized. Fixed effects, pre-trend diagnostics, alternative estimators, and domestic-PhD comparisons do not eliminate faculty-specific contemporaneous changes or endogenous adoption.
- Tenure track is a bundle of contract risk, evaluation rules, promotion pathways, salary, funding, and recruitment changes. Controls and temporal patterns cannot fully identify the causal contribution of each mechanism.
- Publication counts, journal tiers, and SJR weights measure only selected dimensions of research performance; they do not establish novelty, validity, social value, teaching quality, student outcomes, or service contributions.
- The reform initially targeted overseas-trained economists, and the sample is concentrated in productive Chinese economics departments. Effects may differ across disciplines, technology-oriented institutions, university types, and countries.
- The post-tenure decline is consistent with changing evaluation pressure, but publication lags and career-stage changes complicate a direct effort interpretation.
- Recruitment analyses use observable proxies and an inferred revise-and-resubmit date. Unobserved candidate quality may remain.
- Mechanism regressions and faculty surveys do not constitute randomized mediation tests, and perceived hypothetical pressure may differ from pressure actually experienced.
- The reported incumbent spillovers are estimator-sensitive and should not be treated as established positive externalities.
