## TL;DR

Fifteen interviews with independent Chinese technology creators on Douyin and Bilibili show how audience trust and sponsor dependence make ethical negotiation an ongoing form of work. Lu extends [[concepts/relational-labour|Relational Labour]] to a platform-mediated creator–audience–brand triad and calls creators’ shifting disclosure practices and personal boundaries **contingent moral improvisation**. These tactics sustain relationships while leaving ethical and reputational risks with individuals.

## Research Question

How do independent technology creators negotiate competing audience and brand demands under platform mediation, and how do these relationships shape disclosure, ethical decisions and content strategies?

## Motivation

Independent creators combine production, audience engagement and advertising negotiation without newsroom-style editorial–commercial separation. Audience expectations of sincerity conflict with sponsor promotion and platform optimisation. The paper treats this mismatch of [[concepts/moral-economy|Moral Economies]] as a structural source of precarity.

## Contributions

- Conceptualises triadic relational labour, including both relationship maintenance and everyday resistance.
- Defines peripherality through distance from institutional protections and professional ethical frameworks, alongside material dependence.
- Introduces contingent moral improvisation to explain situational disclosure, selective sincerity and personal ethical boundaries.
- Argues that platforms actively structure these relations while individualising responsibility for their contradictions.

## Method

Recruitment combined platform keyword searches and private messages with snowball referrals. Eligibility required independent channel management, at least ten technology videos in the preceding six months, and actual or intended monetisation. Six participants had 10,000–50,000 followers, five had 50,000–100,000, and four had 100,000–500,000. None belonged to MCNs or similar agencies.

Remote Microsoft Teams interviews took place from August to November 2024, lasted 90–150 minutes (mean 118), and were audio-recorded with consent. Reflexive thematic analysis used inductive line-by-line coding, memo writing and iterative theme development with an abductive orientation. Relational labour, moral economies and resistance served as sensitising concepts. Participants received pseudonyms and identifying details were altered.

## Experiments

This qualitative interview study reports interpretive findings rather than controlled experiments.

- **Audience maintenance:** all fifteen creators maintained fan chat groups; eight listed personal WeChat accounts. Moderation, advice, giveaways and responding to criticism supported credibility and visibility, usually yielding indirect rather than direct income.
- **Brand dependence:** device access, launch attendance and personal PR relationships supported production. Accounts of self-funded favourable reviews, anticipatory self-censorship and selective brand allegiance illustrate accommodation to sponsor power.
- **Negotiated authenticity:** one participant described presenting brand-supplied devices as loans from friends; another accepted promotional emphasis while refusing to manipulate comparison photographs. These are reported practices, not independently audited content outcomes.
- **Moral improvisation:** creators described rejecting unsuitable sponsorships, compensating technical criticism with favourable brand narratives, and setting personal boundaries. Peng adopted three unsponsored videos per sponsored video to manage his ethical discomfort.
- **Limits of resistance:** the study found individual coping and tactical resistance but no collective responses among interviewees. The author interprets short-term relational stability as compatible with persistent structural subordination.

## Limitations

The small, purposive and snowball sample supports situated interpretation rather than population prevalence or causal estimates. Evidence comes from creators’ accounts, without independent audience or advertiser interviews establishing their perspectives. Generalisation across genres, platforms and national contexts remains open. Agency-managed creators fall outside the sample. Proposed collective associations and shared disclosure norms are recommendations, not evaluated interventions. Interview data are restricted for confidentiality, with suitably anonymised materials potentially available on request.
